## CHAPTER 15: THE PRICE OF A ROOM

Odin was gone before light, and he left his instructions with the innkeeper instead of with them, which Diep understood was the lesson.

The innkeeper delivered it over breakfast with the air of a man reciting something he had been made to repeat back twice. "He says: you are not to leave the valley for one month. He says: the boy is to be hit until he stops flinching, and he did not say which boy. He says the girl is to spend what she was given." He put down a pot of something grey and steaming. "He also says you owe me for the room, which he did not pay for, and which he told me to tell you he could have."

"That's a god," Miss Hue said.

"That's most of them, ma'am."

The grey stuff turned out to be barley porridge with fat in it. Cuong ate two bowls standing at the window with his back to the room and his eyes on the street, which was his way of sitting down. Mei ate nothing. Diep put a bowl beside her pallet, and Mei said thank you through the wire of her jaw, and Diep left before either of them had to do anything else about it.

---

The Kettle's common room filled at midday and Diep learned more in two hours of sitting in it than she had in four days of walking behind a god.

Wall's Rest was not one town. It was three towns wearing one palisade, and the seams showed if you watched who sat where.

There were the valley people, who had been here for generations and who came in at noon smelling of animals and left by dark. They talked about water rights and about a bridge, and they had a way of not looking directly at anyone in a livery coat.

There were the coat-wearers, a dozen of them, who worked for a man called the Warden of the Eastern Line who lived four days away in Leath and had never once been seen in Wall's Rest. They ran the wire, the tax, and the gate, and the valley people paid them and hated them with the calm hatred of people who had been paying for a long time.

And there were the newcomers. There were a great many newcomers. They slept in a tent field outside the south wall that had been there long enough for the tents to be repaired with other tents, and they were from three villages further east, and they had come with what they could carry when the border moved.

"Border doesn't move," a valley man said, at the next table, to nobody. "Goblinkind doesn't want this valley. Never has. They want the pass and they want the pass because we shut it."

"Tell it to the tents," said his friend.

Diep listened. She had a copper on the table in front of her that she had earned by helping a woman lift a churn, and she made it last three hours, and nobody talked to her, which was fine, because everybody talked around her.

She learned that a claim was a thing you had to defend and that nobody here had ever seen a champion do it.

She learned that champions came through Wall's Rest maybe twice a year, always going east, and that they were mostly polite and mostly frightening.

She learned, from a drunk under the stairs who was talking to a dog, that a champion party had come through in the spring, seven of them, dark-skinned and speaking a language nobody knew, and they had bought every arrow in the valley and paid over the odds for it.

"Where'd they go?" Diep asked.

The drunk looked at her, and looked at the dog, and the dog offered nothing.

"East," he said. "Everybody goes east." He drank. "Nobody comes back west, though, do they. You'd think one of them would come back west just for the once."

---

The trouble started when she tried to buy rope.

Not for any reason. Cuong had said, over the porridge, in the flat voice, that four days from a border with no rope and no oil and eleven arrows between them was not a plan, and Diep had agreed out loud in front of everyone, and had then discovered that agreeing out loud in front of everyone meant she was the one who went and did it.

The rope seller was a broad woman with a shop that was mostly a shed. She had a good coil of it, two fingers thick, and she named a price, and Diep counted out her allotment coins.

Then the woman looked past her at Cuong, who had come along because he came along, and stopped in the middle of taking the money.

"He with you?"

"He's my brother."

"He's carrying a judgment."

"He's carrying two."

The rope seller put the coil back on the shelf.

"Not in my shop," she said, and she was not being cruel about it, that was the strange part, she said it the way you say a thing that everyone knows. "Nothing personal to you, girl. Debtor takes goods on credit anywhere in this valley, the balance follows the goods. Man's in arrears with the court, the court can come at what he's touched." She wiped her hands. "I sold a plough to a debtor eleven years ago. I am still paying for that plough."

"He's not buying it. I am."

"He's standing there."

Diep looked at the rope. She looked at her brother, who had already turned to go, because he had been turning to go since the sergeant's *ah* at the gate.

"Wait outside," Diep said.

He waited outside. She bought the rope, and paid four coppers more than the first price because the woman had gone nervous, and she carried it out herself, and neither of them said one word about it all the way back up the hill.

---

That evening she found the sergeant on the gate and asked for work.

The sergeant's name was Bellim. She had a stool she did not sit on, a ledger she never closed, and a way of looking at people that Diep recognized from every referee she had ever fought under.

"Champions want paid work," Bellim said. "That's new."

"We want to eat past the week."

"Mm." Bellim turned a page in the ledger without looking at it. "There's a thing. It's not a champion's thing. It's a nuisance."

"Tell me the nuisance."

"Sheep." Bellim let that sit, watching her. "Upper terraces. Something is taking sheep, and it is taking them in a way that means it is not a wolf, and the men who went up on the seventh have not come down. That is nine days. Their wives have come to me twice." She turned another page. "The Warden's office says it is a stock matter and stock matters are the valley's own affair. So it is nobody's, so it is yours, if you want it. Two silver, paid out of the town's purse, which I keep in a box, which I will show you is not very full."

Diep thought about the tents outside the south wall and about the woman with the yoke and forty steps and no rest.

"Two silver," she said.

"And you tell me true what you find," Bellim said. "Not what makes a story. I do not need a story. I have three hundred sheep and eleven men on this wall and I need to know what is on my terraces."

"Yes."

Bellim looked at her a moment longer.

"You are how old?"

"Nineteen."

"Mm," said Bellim, and did not say the rest of it, and wrote something in the ledger, and that was the first work the Champion of Vietnam was ever hired to do.

---

Diep told them in the long room with the window, standing up, because sitting down to give an order had felt wrong the one time she tried it.

"Sheep," Cuong said.

"Nine days of missing men."

"Sheep," said Miss Hue, from her pallet, with enormous satisfaction. "Good. You will make every mistake you are going to make, and you will make them where the stakes are sheep, and then later when the stakes are not sheep you will already have made them." She sat up. "I have watched two wars start with someone's goats. Do not let your brother go first."

"He's going to go first."

"Then say it before he does, so it is an order and not a habit."

Diep looked at Cuong. Cuong looked back at her with the mild attentive expression of a man who has been waiting four days to be given something to do.

"You go first," she said. "But you stop when I say stop, and you don't get in front of Mei."

"Mei has one arm."

"That's why."

Something moved at the far end of the room. Mei had rolled over on her pallet and was looking at them, at Diep, with her wrecked face, and her mouth opened as if she was going to say something.

She did not say it. She lay back down and faced the wall.

The screen came up in front of Diep's eyes as she was pulling on her boots, unasked.
