## CHAPTER 2: THE MASSACRE AT PHU NHUAN

The market exploded with violence.

No warning. No demand. One moment Cuong was raising his hand to Duy across the lane, and the next, bullets sprayed into the crowd, sweeping left and right at waist height.

People went down before the sound reached them.

The tall young man who ducked his head every morning to get into the noodle stall took two rounds through the back. His legs kept walking after the rest of him stopped, and he went face first through the stall's front table, and the table came down on top of him along with forty bowls.

The fish seller took one in the hip and one under the arm. She spun into her own counter and folded over it backward, and her brass scale went up out of her hands and came down on the tile beside her head, ringing, spilling her morning's take across the wet stones.

Miss Hue was forty meters away with her market bag on her arm. A burst meant for somebody else walked across her and took the top of her head off. Her body stayed upright a moment longer, hand still closed on the bag, and then the strings went and she dropped straight down where she stood.

Chị Ba, who had fought the French, killed by a stray round on her way to buy fish.

A bullet went through Cuong's left shoulder and put him down in the fruit stall.

He landed hard among the dragonfruit, legs tangled in a crate.

Six months ago he had traded fire with two men in a stairwell in District 8, and it had gotten him the detail, and he had walked around ever since believing he knew what a gunfight was.

He had never been shot before. That was the part nobody could teach him. It went through him like a truck and kept going, and for a few seconds he was not a police officer at all, just a young man lying in fruit with his mouth open, trying to work out where his arm had gone.

Two meters away, Mei hit Diep at the waist and took her off her feet and out of the burst, both of them going down behind the stall's low wall.

Cuong got his right elbow under himself and thought, with the last clean part of his mind, that Mei had just saved his sister's life.

Across the lane, through stalls going over and a stampede of running legs, Cuong found Duy. The first burst had caught him through the hip, and he was hauling himself toward the spring roll vendor's steel table on one elbow, his other hand clamped over the hole, leaving a wet track across the tile behind him.

Alive, then. Moving.

Cuong took stock the way the trainers drilled it, and got about half of it.

Arm dead from the shoulder down. Bullet through and out the other side. Not going to die of it in the next minute, which was as far ahead as he could see.

His right hand was shaking hard enough that he had to look at it twice to be sure it was still holding the Beretta. The left hand was not going to help hold anything, which meant one-handed from here, the thing the range drills prepare you for and hope you never need.

He keyed the radio one-handed and gave the call in the flat voice they trained into you, shots fired, Phu Nhuan central market, officers down, and the word officers came out plural because the arithmetic was already visible from where he lay.

Duy was firing around the steel table one-handed, the other hand still clamped on his hip, face gone gray, grin gone with it. The three gangsters were walking their fire into his cover, each burst chewing a little closer, with the patience of men who had done this before.

Cuong got his right elbow onto a crate, the brace they teach for a dead support arm, and went to work.

The lead man crossed the lane at a run. Cuong shot him three times in the chest. He sat down against a vegetable cart like a drunk.

The second turned to find the new threat. Cuong shot him twice in the back. No honor owed.

The third went to ground behind an overturned cart and did not come up.

One breath. Screaming, and sirens somewhere too far away.

He turned his head to find them behind the low wall, his sister and the girl who had pulled her down.

"Mei," he said. "Thank you. Thank you for"

Diep was not moving. Mei knelt over her with both hands low on Diep's stomach, the way you kneel to hold pressure on a wound. Blood pumped out around her fingers. Her right hand still held the knife.

"I'm so sorry," Mei said. She was crying. She did not let go of the knife. "They have Van."

Cuong shot her twice and felt nothing at all, and understood dimly that the nothing was a wound of its own, and that he was not going to live long enough for it to matter.

He crawled. It took a very long time, longer than the shooting had taken, his legs working and his left side dragging and his hand slipping in the wet, and when he got there he put his good hand where Mei's hands had been and pressed down.

Diep's eyes tracked him. Her mouth made the shape of his name, and blood came instead of it.

"I know," he said. "I know. I'm here. I've got you."

The third gangster came out from behind his cart when the shooting stopped, and crossed the lane at a walk.

Duy came up from behind the steel table with his gun out and blood sheeting down his leg, shouting something that was a warning or a promise. The gangster turned and put three rounds into him at four meters. Duy folded against the steel and stayed there.

Then the man turned and walked toward the fruit stall, taking his time about it.

Cuong watched him come and stayed where he was. His hand was on his sister. He was very cold, and his fingers had gone clumsy, and blood kept coming up around them however hard he leaned.

The first round hit him under the collarbone and turned him halfway over. The second went into his chest and stayed there.

The sky over Phu Nhuan went a beautiful hard blue.

Cuong dragged his hand back onto his sister. It was already too late to matter and he pressed down anyway. She breathed in small wet pieces underneath it. His last thought was not a prayer and was addressed to no one in particular.

Let it take me first. I don't want to see.

It took him first.
