## CHAPTER 3: THE VOID

Diep sat up on a slab of marble and the first thing she did was put both hands to her stomach.

The wound was gone. The romper was whole, no blood, no cut, the lotus flowers as white as the morning her mother gave it to her. She pushed her fingers into the place where the knife had gone in, hard, and felt nothing but her own stomach. She did it again. Then she looked at her hands, and her hands were clean, and she wiped them on the romper anyway.

Light stood over her in a column, and beyond it, dark with no walls in it. Four more slabs, four more columns.

Cuong was already on his feet. He had both hands flat against his own chest, pressing, the way she had just pressed her stomach, and he was looking down at the uniform shirt where two holes should have been and were not.

Duy sat with his head in his hands. He had not looked up.

Miss Hue sat cross-legged with her hands folded and her lips moving in prayer, and her market bag was gone, and the top of her head was where it belonged.

And Mei.

Mei was on the last slab, and she was already looking at Diep, and she had been looking at Diep since before Diep opened her eyes. Her hands were folded in her lap. There was nothing in them.

Diep came off the slab.

She crossed the space without any memory of deciding to. Mei was still getting to her feet when Diep hit her, and the nose crumpled and went sideways under her knuckles, and Mei dropped.

She landed on her back and she did not cover up. She lay there with her hands open on the stone and looked up at Diep and waited for the rest of it.

Diep went down on top of her and gave it to her.

Cuong got there a second behind his sister and could not reach past her, so he took Mei by the ankle and hauled her out from under Diep across the floor, and then he was on her, one hand knotted in her shirt, the other coming down.

"You were in our house," he said. "You ate at our table. You were in our house."

"Hey. HEY." Duy came off his slab at a dead run, got his arms around Cuong's chest from behind and pulled, and Cuong took him along like a coat. "Cuong. You're going to kill her."

"She's already dead," Cuong said. "We're all already dead."

Diep crawled to them and got Mei's arm.

She took it the way the master had taught her when she was eleven, wrist and elbow and shoulder lined up, her hip as the fulcrum.

"I bought corn for you." Her voice tore coming out. "Forty people. I got up at six for you."

She leaned.

The arm broke with a sound like a green branch.

Mei screamed and did not fight back.

"It was six forty."

Miss Hue had not raised her voice and had not gotten off her slab. She said it the way she had said everything for ninety-two years, and everyone in the room heard it.

Diep's head came up.

"Master Thanh did not spend eleven years teaching you to be a liar," Miss Hue said. "He did not teach you this either, but I imagine he would understand it. He was not a patient man about certain things."

Nobody moved.

"Let go of her arm, Diep. It is already broken. It will not get more broken and you will not feel better." Miss Hue folded her hands. "And she cannot answer questions with her jaw in pieces. I have questions. So do you. So does your brother, though he has not thought of them yet, because he is busy."

Diep let go.

She sat back on the stone and her hands were shaking and there was blood on them that was not hers, and Mei rolled onto her side and made a noise like a hurt animal and could not stop.

"They have Van." It came out of her in pieces, through a mouth that was not working right. "Four months ago. Men came to the house. Then they sent an envelope, and Diep, there were fingers in it. Two of his fingers. And a phone number."

Nobody said anything.

"I called them. They put his face on a video so I would know he was alive, and then they told me the price." Mei got her good hand under herself and pushed up far enough to see them. "The price was you. You and your brother, in the market, on a morning they picked."

She was crying so hard the words came apart.

"I did it. I knew. I knew the whole time." She got a breath. "He's my brother. He's my brother, Diep."

Her head went down onto the stone.

"Kill me again. You can. We're already dead, it doesn't count, so do it, one of you, please do it."

"They killed him anyway," Diep said. "Or they didn't. Either way you got nothing."

"I know."

"You slept three meters from me for two years." Diep wiped her hands on the lotus flowers and left long red marks. "You should have handed me the envelope."

"I know," Mei said.

Duy had not let go of Cuong. He stood behind him with his arms still locked around his partner's chest, and his face over Cuong's shoulder had gone the color of paper.

"Somebody tell me," he said. "I was across the lane. I saw you shoot her, Cuong, I saw it and I didn't, somebody tell me what she did."

Nobody answered him.

"She put a knife in my sister," Cuong said finally, "while I was thanking her for saving my sister's life."

Duy let go of him.

"Well," said a voice. "That was about how I figured that would go."

It came from everywhere, an easy drawl, and a man was suddenly present in the way furniture is present, as if he had always been standing there and the darkness had only now admitted it. Tall, bearded, a shepherd's crook in one hand, a battered hat pushed back on his head. Boots with spurs. He looked like a film their father used to make them watch on Sunday afternoons. Diep got up off the floor and stood between him and the others, with her bloody hands loose at her sides.

"Y'all have questions. Natural as rain. Let me save us some time." He counted on his fingers. "One. Yes, you're dead. Every one of you, this morning, violently, and I am sorry for it, that was ugly work. Two. No, this is not the afterlife. The afterlife is that direction."

He nodded at the dark, and the dark opened onto a distant arch of black stone lit gold at its edges, and a line of people before it that went back farther than seeing. Thousands. More. Waiting with the patience of the already dead.

Miss Hue came up off her slab halfway, searching the nearest faces in that line, and lowered herself back down without a word.

"Three. You are not in that line, because I pulled you out of it, because I am going to offer you a job."

"Who are you?" Cuong asked.

"Depends on the century. Lately, most folks say Adonai."

Miss Hue's prayer stopped. She looked at the man in the hat for a long moment, and then, being Miss Hue, she bowed politely and kept her opinions in reserve.

"Ma'am." He took the hat off for that one, and held it. "Bless you for the prayers. Come here a moment, if you would. I don't send anybody where you're going without provisions, and you have been shortchanged on time more than most."

Gold light gathered around the old woman like dust finding a sunbeam. It built, spun, and unwound itself into a neat spool on the floor.

Where Miss Hue had been sat a woman of perhaps thirty. Tall. Black hair in a knot held with ebony sticks. Small overlapping plates of armor over a yellow dress, and a morningstar across her knees.

The same eyes. The same set of the mouth. She did not look surprised.

Miss Hue looked down at her own hands, turned them over, and flexed them.

"Ninety-two years," she said, in a voice with all the gravel washed out of it, "I earned every one, and you will give me an accounting later for which parts of me you kept." She lifted the morningstar, weighed it, and set it down. "This is acceptable."

"Yes ma'am," said the god, meekly.

He looked over at Mei then, at the arm and the face and the blood on the stone, and something went out of the drawl.

"That I can't undo," he said. "Not here. You'll carry that across with you, girl, and I'm sorry, and I'd say you know why."

Mei nodded. She did not ask him to try.

Writing appeared in the air.

It hung in front of Diep's eyes at a comfortable distance, blue-bordered, patient, and when she moved her head it moved with her, courteous as a waiter.

> **Provisional System access: granted.**
> Subject: Diep Loc. Status: deceased. Cause: exsanguination. Instrument: a blade.
> The blade belonged to a friend. This is recorded under aggravating factors.

Across the slabs she saw the others reading their own floating text, and she saw Mei close her eyes.

> **Notice of situation, abbreviated at the request of your recruiter.**
> The pocket universe containing Earth will fail in approximately one year. Upon failure, Earth rejoins the multiverse. Projected human survival of unassisted reintegration: it is the System's policy not to display this figure to the recently deceased.

> **Offer of employment.**
> Position: Champion of Earth (1), Companions (up to 4). Posting: Atlantis. Duties: survive, establish, ally, defend.
> Compensation: a second life, effective immediately.
> Refusal: the line is over there. It does not move quickly, but it moves.

Diep read it twice. Her hands had stopped shaking. She noticed that, and did not know what to do with it.

Across from her, Duy was reading his own column with his mouth slightly open.

"It knows about my mother," he said, to nobody. "How does it know about my mother."

"A year," Cuong said. "You're telling us the world ends in a year, and your plan is five dead people from Phu Nhuan."

"Two hundred and one teams," Adonai said. "One per nation. You'd be Vietnam." The drawl thinned as he said it, just for a moment, and underneath it was something old and very tired. "And no, son, it is not a good plan. It is the plan the law allows me. I built the wall around your world myself, two thousand years back, and I built it wrong, and everything you're about to suffer flows downhill from that. So I don't ask you to trust me. I ask you to work."

Thunder answered him.

It struck out of nothing, close enough that Diep felt it in her teeth, and standing in the afterimage was a mountain wearing wolf fur, one-eyed, carrying a spear, a raven the size of a dog riding his shoulder. He looked at the five of them the way a horse trader looks at horses.

"You're coddling them again," the newcomer said.

"I'm briefing them, Odin."

"You're apologizing to them. Your last five briefed teams are dead or worse." The one eye moved across them, slab by slab, and stopped on nobody, which was somehow worse than being singled out. "You want to know what's true, Vietnam? Everything he said, plus this. The multiverse does not care how you died or who owes whom what. It will weigh you, and it starts weighing now."

"Odin." Adonai's voice dropped its costume entirely. "They haven't accepted. They haven't been trained. They don't even know how to read their own"

Odin struck the butt of his spear against the floor.

The world went out like a lamp.
