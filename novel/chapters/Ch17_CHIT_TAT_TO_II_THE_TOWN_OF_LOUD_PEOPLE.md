## CHAPTER 17: CHIT-TAT-TO II: THE TOWN OF LOUD PEOPLE

The tall folk built everything twice as big as it needed to be, and Chit-tat-to had been watching them do it for two days from a drainage culvert under their south wall.

The culvert was good. It was dry in the top half and it ran from the tent field down under the palisade into the creek, and it was exactly the size a person should be, which meant the tall folk had built it for water and not for people, which meant nobody came down it.

He had made a nest in the high corner out of his mother's basket and Ptik's fishing line and a piece of sacking he had taken off a fence. He had eaten four of their chickens' eggs and one thing from a rubbish pile that he regretted.

And he had watched.

This was the thing Chit-tat-to could do, the only thing, the thing that made him useless at fishing and useless at digging and useful exactly once, in a dark room, when everyone else had been running. He could sit still and look at something until it made sense.

So he sat still and looked at a town.

---

What he learned, in order:

The tall folk had three kinds. One kind smelled of animals and came in at the middle of the day and left before dark. One kind wore the same coat as each other and stood where the gates were and took things from the first kind. And one kind lived in the tents outside the wall and was not allowed in after dark, and was hungry, and had children who were also hungry.

Nobody fed the tent people. That surprised him. Sixty-two people in his village and if one family had a bad season the whole village ate a little less, because that was simply what you did with a bad season.

The coat kind counted things. All day. Sacks, animals, people through the gate, and they wrote it down in books, and Chit-tat-to had never seen writing done by a hand before and he watched the marks appear for an entire afternoon without blinking much.

And on the second morning, the ones he had come for walked out of the gate.

---

He knew them instantly, and his body tried to run and he did not let it.

The male one first, the one from the light at the end of the room, walking with a black stick in his hand and his head turning the way a heron's turns. Behind him a tall woman, gold somewhere under her skin, with a weapon on her shoulder that Chit-tat-to disliked at a level below thinking.

Then the one with the broken arm bound up, walking slowly.

And then the female one.

She came out last and she was smaller than he remembered. That was the thing that undid him for a moment, crouched in the mouth of a drainage culvert with his heart going: in the room in the deep she had been enormous, a shape of noise and light, and here she came through a wooden gate in the morning being about as tall as a Ratkin door.

There were marks in the air over all of them.

Chit-tat-to could see the marks because he could see the picture-screens, and he understood that this was not something the tall folk around them could do, because nobody in the town looked up at the marks, ever, not once in two days.

He could not read the marks. But he had been looking at the coat-people's books all afternoon, and he had learned one thing about writing, which is that the same mark means the same thing every time.

So when he saw the mark over the male one's head, he knew it.

It was the word from his own screen. The six-mark word under the picture of the scales. The one that had been following him since the room in the deep.

It was over that man's head, and everyone who passed him in the gateway looked at it and then made a small space around him without seeming to decide to.

Chit-tat-to sat back in the dark of the culvert with his tail curled over his feet.

*The word is his,* he thought. *The word is what he is. And the scales were under it.*

He did not know what the word said. But he knew now that whatever the word meant, the multiverse had written it on that man where everyone could see, and the town had read it, and the town had answered by stepping back.

That was a thing you could work with. He did not have any idea yet how. But it was a thing.

---

They went up toward the terraces and were gone two days, and on the evening of the second day they came back down carrying the one with the broken arm on a litter, and Chit-tat-to made a decision.

It was not a brave decision. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, because the songs would say brave if there were anyone left to make a song, and it had not been brave. It had been the arithmetic of a person with nothing.

Sixty-two people were hiding in a tunnel with bad air. The tall folk who had killed nineteen of his village had a word over the head of the one who did it, and the word meant something owed, and Chit-tat-to had spent two days failing to think of a single other way to ask for what was owed.

So the decision was: go and be seen.

He would very probably be killed. He had watched the coat-people kill a dog in the street on his first morning for being where it was not supposed to be, unhurriedly, with a stick, and nobody had even stopped walking.

He ate the last egg. He put his mother's basket on his back, because he was not going to be found without it. He waited until the town went quiet.

Then he came up out of the culvert into the tent field with his hands open and empty at his sides, and he walked toward the light of the low fire where the tall folk were sitting.

---

The old woman saw him first.

She did not shout. She was sitting with her back to a cart with the terrible weapon across her knees, and she looked up, and she looked straight at him standing at the edge of the firelight, and she did not move at all.

Then she said something, quietly, to the others.

The male one came up off the ground fast. The black stick came up with him, and Chit-tat-to's whole body screamed at him to run, and he stayed, and his legs shook so badly he had to lock his knees.

He kept his hands open. He kept them where they could be seen. He had thought about that part for two days.

The female one stood up more slowly, and she put one hand out flat toward the male one without looking at him, and the black stick went down, not all the way, but down.

Nobody said anything.

Chit-tat-to had prepared for this. He had spent two nights in a culvert preparing for exactly this, arranging it in his head in pictures in the right order, and now that it was happening he could feel all of the pictures trying to come out of his mouth at once in a language none of them had.

So he did what he had done at the fire, because it was all he had.

He pointed at the male one. Then he held up both hands, fingers spread, and closed them and opened them, and did it again, and again, and once more with four fingers down. Nineteen.

Then he pointed at the ground between them and held his hand out flat, palm up.

*This is owed.*

The female one made a sound. It was small and it was not a word, and she put her hand over her mouth.

And then, because he had rehearsed one more thing and he was going to do all of it before anyone could decide anything, Chit-tat-to reached slowly behind himself and took his mother's carry-basket off his back and set it down on the ground in front of him, on its side, so they could see that it was empty.

He had thought a long time about how to say *I have nothing left and I am asking anyway* to people who did not share a single word with him.

That was the best he had.

The old woman got up. She did it the way old people get up, even in her new body, and she came around the fire and crouched down in front of him at his own height, which nobody that size had ever done in front of him in his life.

She looked at the basket. She looked at his hands. She looked at his face for a long moment.

Then she reached over and turned the basket upright, and set it on the ground between them, and she took something out of her own pack and put it in the basket.

It was a piece of bread.

Chit-tat-to looked at the bread in his mother's basket, and he sat down in the dirt in front of four people who had killed his family and cried like the child he had stopped being eleven days ago.
