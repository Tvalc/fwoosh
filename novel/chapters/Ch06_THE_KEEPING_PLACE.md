## CHAPTER 6: THE KEEPING PLACE

Cuong went through the door low, with the Beretta up in both hands, and his habits were all he had left.

The Beretta led into a corridor of the same wet black stone as the cell, torch brackets at intervals a taller people had set. He moved along it as he had been taught, weapon and eyes arriving at each corner together.

None of it meant anything anymore, because the numbers over his head said what he was now. A rat the size of a hound had come at Duy from a drain before they moved out, and Cuong had put it down with one shot, clean, with two working arms and no pain anywhere in him, and the screen that followed had shown him his future.

> Garnished: 36. Held.
> Net: 4.

Four.

"Doors," Duy said behind him. "Cuong. Look at the doors."

The corridor was lined with them. Cell doors, iron-strapped, and each one stood open, and each one was small. Not small like cheap. Small like the people they were made for. Waist-high to Cuong, with waist-high shackle points inside, and straw in the corners, and in the straw of the third cell a carved wooden comb the length of his thumb, with three teeth broken.

Cuong crouched at the cell mouth with the comb in his hand and made himself look back down the corridor, at the row they had dragged into the dark, and see them properly for the first time.

Sleek dark fur, dust-gray at the muzzles on the older ones. Rounded ears. Hands, actual hands, four-fingered and clever, one of them still closed around a stone the size of a plum, which was the whole of that person's armory.

The tallest of them came up to his chest. One had been wearing a braided cord of red thread at the throat, dyed, knotted, made by somebody for somebody. The comb in Cuong's hand had been carved by a person who had time, in a cell, and wanted to be beautiful.

Nobody spoke.

"They weren't a warband," Diep said finally. Her voice did the thing it had done since the market, starting somewhere steady and finishing somewhere else. "The quest said save them. They were running from here. They were running from something, out of their cells, toward the first open door in, in however long, and the first thing through it was us."

"The first thing through it was gunfire," Miss Hue said. She stood in the small doorway with her morningstar resting on her shoulder and looked at the comb for a long time. "Say it correctly or don't say it."

Mei crouched at the lock of the fourth cell and turned it gently with two fingers. "These are feeding locks," she said. It was the first full sentence she had volunteered since the slabs. "You can open them from outside without stopping what's inside from being fed. My father sold cages. I know what a farm looks like."

Cuong looked down the corridor, where it bent left and descended, and where the dragging sound came from. Holding cells. Feeding locks. Something that kept things.

"It kept them," he said. "Something kept them here, and they got out, and it is going to come see about its stock."

At the bend of the corridor, sunk into the ceiling in grooves green with age, hung a portcullis, a gate of crossed iron bars made to fall and seal the passage, its chain running to a rusted crank on the wall. Cuong stood under it and looked up at it.

"Everyone remember this gate," he said.

From below the bend, closer than before, the dragging came again, and with it, for the first time, a sound like keys.
