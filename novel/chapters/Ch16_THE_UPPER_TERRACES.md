## CHAPTER 16: THE UPPER TERRACES

They found the first sheep an hour above the town, and it had not been eaten.

It lay in the short grass with its legs folded under it and its throat opened and nothing else touched. Cuong crouched over it with the iron stave across his knees and did not put his hand on it.

"Two days," he said. "Maybe three, in this cold."

"How do you know that?"

"I don't. I know what two days looks like on people." He stood. "The wool's wrong."

It was. Diep saw it once he said it. The fleece along the animal's back was pressed flat in two long grooves, shoulder to hip, as if something had held it down from above while it worked.

Miss Hue leaned on the morningstar and looked at the grooves for a while.

"Put that in your head and keep it there," she said. "Whatever is up here has hands."

---

The terraces went up the valley's eastern wall in shelves, each one walled in dry stone, each one smaller than the last. Sheep tracks everywhere. Water running down through cut channels somebody had been maintaining for four hundred years.

They found the second sheep, and the third, both the same. Neither eaten.

They found the men on the fourth terrace, in the herders' hut.

Cuong went in first because Diep had said he could. He came out fast and put his back against the stones beside the door.

"Don't," he said, to Diep, who had started forward. "You don't need it."

"Bellim needs it."

"Then I'll tell her."

"She asked me." Diep heard her own voice go flat and steady over the top of something that was not steady at all. "She asked for what's true, and I'm not sending my brother in to look so I can keep my breakfast."

He stood aside.

There were three of them in the hut. They had gone in and barred the door from the inside, which had been sensible, and then something had come through the roof, which had not been anticipated by whoever built that hut.

Diep made herself count. Three. She made herself look at the wounds, which were not bites. She made herself notice that one man still held his crook, and that the crook had been snapped near the top, and that the broken end lay in the corner eight feet away.

Then she went out and was sick behind the hut where only Mei could see her, and Mei looked away and let her have it.

"Three men," Diep said when she came back. "Barred the door. It came through the roof. Not eaten, same as the sheep."

"Then it climbs," Cuong said.

"It climbs, it has hands, and it isn't hungry." Miss Hue's face had gone somewhere Diep did not like. "I have known one kind of thing that kills a great deal and eats none of it."

"Which is?"

"Something that is frightened of something else."

---

Diep stopped them on the eighth terrace and made them stand in a circle in the wind, because she had been thinking the whole way up and thinking was no good in her head alone.

"It's above us and it knows we're here," she said. "So we pick the ground and we pick the order. Cuong first, Miss Hue on his left, me right. Mei behind the wall with the rope and the poles, and she stays there."

"Mei should be four terraces down," Cuong said.

"Mei can't make four terraces down alone with that arm."

"Then someone walks her."

"Then we're three." Diep did not look away from him. "We're not three. We're four and a hurt one, and I would rather have the hurt one where I can see her than out on this hill by herself with that thing between us."

Nobody argued. Mei said nothing at all, which she had been doing for six days.

Diep opened her hand at Cuong. "Say the thing back to me."

"Which thing."

"The four seconds."

His jaw set. "If you get hit, I stop seeing the rest of the field."

"For how long?"

"I don't know."

"Then we find out today," Diep said, "and we find out on purpose."

---

It came over the wall on their right in one motion, and the party came apart the way five people do when four meters of anything lands in the middle of them.

Diep got a picture that stayed with her. Too many joints in the arms. A flat wet face with the eyes set wide like a goat's. A belly gone hollow and ribbed under grey-white fur.

It did not go for the biggest of them. It went for Mei.

Mei went down under it with her broken arm trapped in the sling between her body and its weight, and she made a sound through her wired jaw that did not have a word in it.

"Cuong, left side, get it off her."

Cuong was already moving, and it was already going wrong, because Diep moved too. She went in at its flank with both knives out of instinct, twelve years of instinct, in under the arm the way she went in under everyone.

The long limb came around and caught her across the ribs and put her into the dry-stone wall.

The sky changed places with the ground.

And Cuong stopped.

Diep saw it from the base of the wall with her ears ringing. Her brother, four meters from a thing sitting on Mei's chest, standing still with the stave in both hands and his whole face aimed at his sister. Not frozen with fear. Frozen with attention. Every part of him pointed one way, and nothing else in the world getting through.

Miss Hue hit the creature's shoulder with the morningstar and it barely turned its head.

Diep spat blood into the grass and got her elbow under her, and two things arrived at once, and the second one was useful.

The first was that she had done this to them. She had gone in, because going in was what she was, and the moment she was hit her brother had become a statue four meters from the person who needed him.

The second was that the System had told her how to fix it, in writing, six days ago, and she had read it as a curse instead of a manual.

*While a wounded ally is within your reach.*

"CUONG." She got her feet under her, and she did not run at the creature. She ran at Mei. She dropped on her knees beside Mei's head, inside the thing's reach, close enough to feel the heat coming off it, and she got both hands onto the tear in Mei's shoulder and pressed down until blood came up between her fingers. "HERE. I'M HERE, AND SHE'S BLEEDING, AND I NEED YOU HERE."

Her brother arrived.

The stave came around at the height of a man's knee and the creature's leg folded the wrong way. It shrieked and reared off Mei's chest. Cuong did not step back to swing again. He planted himself over both women with his boots between them and it, and the next blow took it across the flat wet face, and the one after that took its good arm.

His hands were steady. Diep, kneeling in the blood with her palms on her friend, watched her brother's hands stop shaking for the first time since the market.

"Miss Hue. Left side, he's holding it."

The old woman came in on the blind flank and put the morningstar into its knee. The creature went down onto the terrace wall and took ten feet of four-hundred-year-old stonework with it, and then it was scrambling backward up the slope on three limbs with its ruined arm swinging.

"Let it go," Diep said.

Cuong stood over them with the stave up, breathing, and let it go.

The screens came while nobody was ready for them.

> Engagement resolved: no kill. Hostile withdrew.
> Experience awarded: 240.
> Garnished: 216. Held.
> Net: 24.

> Effect active this engagement: Tunnel. Duration: 3.4 seconds.
> Effect active this engagement: Steady Hands.
> The System notes that the second effect was induced deliberately by the Champion.
> The System has no ruling on this. The System is making a note of it.

Diep read the last line three times.

"It's a tool," she said. "It's not a fault. It's a tool, and tools have handles, and the handle is that if you stop seeing everything except your family, then I stand where the wounded are."

"You knelt inside its reach," Cuong said.

"Yes."

"On purpose."

"Yes."

He looked at her for a long moment, and what was on his face was not gratitude.

"Don't make me into a machine you operate," he said. "I'll do it. That's the problem. You point me and I will go, every time, forever, and one day you will point me at something you should not, and I will not be able to tell you."

Diep did not have an answer for that.

Miss Hue, who had been working on Mei's shoulder the whole time with her sleeves pushed up, spoke without looking at either of them.

"Good," she said. "Both of you. That is the argument you were going to have in a year. Have it now, while the stakes are sheep."

---

They found the reason on the ground where the creature had been.

It had left a track of blood down the terrace, and in the blood, worked out of its flank by the stave, lay a length of black metal as long as Diep's forearm. Barbed. Snapped off at the base.

Not iron. It weighed almost nothing.

"That's not a hunter's," Cuong said. He turned it in the light. "Look at the head. It isn't made to kill what it goes into. It's made to stay in and hurt, so the animal keeps running."

Miss Hue took it from him and went still in a way that made Diep's neck prickle.

"I have seen this."

"Where?"

"On my slab." She handed it back. "There is a mark on the base, under the blood. I saw one like it in the sorting room, on the inside of the arch, where the dead were queued. I looked at it a long while, because I was looking at everything, because I had not expected to be looking at anything ever again."

Diep scraped the base with her thumbnail. Under the blood there was a small stamped figure. A set of scales, one pan heavy, one pan empty.

"Somebody shot that thing with equipment that has a maker's mark," Cuong said. "Equipment means a supply. A supply means a plan." He looked up the terraces toward the dark of the pass. "Nobody's hunting up there. Somebody is driving things down into an inhabited valley on purpose, and this is where they land."

Diep put the barb in her belt. It weighed almost nothing, and she carried it down the mountain like a stone.
