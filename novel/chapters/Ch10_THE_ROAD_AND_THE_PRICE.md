## CHAPTER 10: THE ROAD AND THE PRICE

The waystation keeper's smile died in the exact second his eyes went above Cuong's head.

It was the third day on the pale road, and the station was the first roofed place they had seen, a low stone house with a fire going and a stable smell and two travelers already at the hearth, tall gray-skinned people who stopped talking when the humans came in. The keeper was a round, quick-eyed man of the same gray people, and he had begun the professional warmth of innkeepers everywhere, and then he read Cuong, and the warmth shut like the cashbox.

"Beds are six marks," he said. To Diep. Only to Diep. "For him, twenty. Debtors pay surety on the linens."

The room got quiet in an interested way. The travelers at the hearth had turned to watch, because apparently this was worth watching everywhere in the universe.

Diep walked to the counter. Her mother had priced flowers at a stall for forty years on thin margins and perfect courtesy, and Diep had watched her do it since she was four.

"Six for me," she said pleasantly. "Six for the priestess, and I would keep the priestess happy. Six for the quiet one. And six for my brother, because the mark over his head says the court garnishes him, and the court's rate is nine in ten, and a keeper who prices above the court's own sentence is correcting the court." She let that sit. She had read the judgment thirty times in three days; she could recite it like a lesson. "Is that what I should tell them at Threshold? That the station on the pass corrects the court?"

The keeper looked at her. He looked at Miss Hue, who smiled at him the way she had once smiled at a cactus.

"Six," the keeper said. "Marks in advance."

Later, on the night watch, with the fire down to coals and her brother finally, chemically asleep, Diep sat on the doorstep and did not look at Mei, who had come out soundless the way she did everything, and sat down an arm's length away, which was the distance they lived at now.

"You watch like him," Mei said quietly. "You didn't used to."

"Don't," Diep said. Then, because the road was long and the question had been eating her since the marble room: "They shot you. Your own people. You did everything they wanted and they shot you where I could see it."

Mei was silent long enough that the coals settled twice.

"I knew they would," she said. "I watched them decide it before I ever agreed. Men like that don't leave the knife alive, Diep, the knife is the loose thread. I did the count anyway. Van free, on one side. Me and, and the rest, on the other." Her voice stayed level the way a wire stays level, under tension. "I'm not asking you to forgive the count. I'm telling you I did it with open eyes, because you keep looking at me like I was tricked, and it's worse than that, and you should know it's worse."

Diep watched the coals. In the morning they would climb the last of the pass. Somewhere behind them, under a mountain, Duy lay in a room with no door, and somewhere ahead a court had posted her country's name on a docket, and beside her sat the person she had loved most and armed least, telling her the truth at last, one watch too late for it to save anyone.

"Corn," Diep said finally. "At the tất niên. Forty people. I practiced the recipe five times."

Mei made a sound that had a laugh buried in it somewhere, and put her face in her hands, and neither of them said anything else until the stars moved.
