## CHAPTER 14: CHIT-TAT-TO I: THE VILLAGE OF NINETEEN ABSENCES

Chit-tat-to came home along the top of the water pipe, because the top of the water pipe was the fastest way and he had never once been allowed to use it.

He used it now. Nobody was left who would tell him not to.

The pipe ran from the black spring down through the root tunnels to the village, an arm's width of old stone laid by somebody long dead, and he went along it at a flat run with his tail out for balance and his mother's carry-basket bumping empty against his back. Twice he had to jump gaps where the stone had fallen. He made both jumps. Nobody saw him make them.

He was thinking in pictures, because that was how thinking was done.

The picture in front, the one he was running toward, was the village: the smoke hole, the grinding stone, the place where his mother sat to work. The picture behind him, the one that kept climbing up over his shoulder to be looked at again, was the room in the deep.

It went like this, and he could not make it stop going like this.

The cage door, open. The long dark. The others ahead of him and around him, all of them running, his uncle's hand in his fur pushing him low. The tall shapes at the far end with the light behind them. Then the thunder.

The thunder was not a picture. That was the trouble with it. Everything else in his head was a picture and the thunder was a hole where the pictures should be, a white noise-shape with edges, and inside it his aunt going down, and Ptik going down, and the old grandmother who knew all the songs going down with her arms still out behind her holding two of the small ones, and both of the small ones going down as well.

Nineteen.

He had counted them because somebody had to carry the number home, and he was the one still standing, so it was his to carry.

He came off the pipe at the drop-shaft and went down the knotted root by feel, and the smell of home came up at him, wet clay and cooking mash and the sharp green of the moss they grew on the ceiling, and it was so exactly right that for a moment the last two days did not fit inside the world at all.

Then he came out into the common space and everyone was already looking at him.

They had known for a day and a night. Rats know. The village had eighty-one people in it before and now it had sixty-two, and the ones who could count had counted, and the ones who could not count had looked at the doorway every time a shadow crossed it.

His mother was not in the doorway.

He had known she would not be. He had watched her go down in the room in the deep, third from the left, with a piece of the stone floor in her hand because she had picked up the only weapon there was. He had known it for two days and it had been a picture in his head for two days.

It was different, arriving, and finding the doorway empty.

Old Kesh-ka came to him first, walking on both her sticks, and she did not say anything. She put her hand flat on the top of his head, which is what you do for a child, and Chit-tat-to was fifteen and had run the water pipe and carried the number home, and he stood there and let an old woman hold his head still with one hand while he shook.

---

They did the rites at the low fire that night, and they did them without any bodies, which had never happened before in the memory of anyone living.

The rite is simple. For each absence, someone who knew them draws them on the wall in wet ash. Not their face. Their thing: the shape of what they made, or what they carried, or what they were good at, so that anyone who comes to the wall in a hundred years can look at the marks and know what the village used to have.

Ptik got a fishing line, because Ptik could take three fish out of the black spring on a day nobody else could take one.

His aunt got a knot, the complicated one, the one she tied in the roof-lashings that never slipped.

The old grandmother got a spiral for her songs, and then Kesh-ka went back and put a second spiral inside the first, and everyone made the low sound in their throats, because that meant the songs were now unheld, and would have to be learned again from people who only half remembered them.

Chit-tat-to drew his mother himself.

He drew the grinding stone. He drew it badly, because his hands would not do what he told them, and he had to go back twice to make the circle close. Nobody said anything about it. When he was done Kesh-ka reached past him and added one small mark at the edge of the stone, a dot with a curve over it, which was the mark for a person who taught, and Chit-tat-to had not known that his mother had taught anyone anything, and now there was nobody left to ask about it.

Nineteen marks on the wall.

The wall had four hundred years of marks on it. Nineteen new ones, all in a row, all in one night, was a thing the wall had never had to hold before.

---

The talking happened after.

It happened the way talking always happened, everyone in a ring at the low fire, the fire kept small because of the smoke, and the elders speaking in order of age and everyone else speaking when they had something.

Chit-tat-to had never spoken at the fire in his life. He was fifteen. You spoke at the fire when you had done a thing.

He waited a long time.

Kesh-ka spoke first, and what she said was: deeper. There were tunnels below the black spring that nobody had opened in three generations, and the village was small enough now to fit in them, and the tall folk did not go down where the air was bad. The village had moved before. In her mother's time they had moved twice. You survive by being where the boots are not.

Almost everyone made the sound for agreement.

Chit-tat-to stood up.

The ring went quiet, not out of respect, out of the surprise of the thing, and he stood there with his hands opening and closing and every picture in his head arriving at once and no way to get any of them out of his mouth in an order that would work.

So he did what he could do. He went to the wall, and he pointed at his mother's grinding stone, and then he held up his hand, fingers spread, and closed it and opened it four times short of nineteen and then made the flat sign for *and more*.

Then he pointed at the floor, meaning here, meaning down, meaning the deep tunnels Kesh-ka wanted.

Then he shook his head.

"Then what," said Rul, who was thirty and had lost a brother in the room in the deep, and who said it not unkindly. "You want what, Chit? You want to go up and knock on the tall folk's door and ask them for something?"

Chit-tat-to thought about that.

The tall folk. The thunder. The two of them in the light at the end of the room, the male one shouting, the female one going flat to the ground with her hands over her head. He had seen that. Everyone else had been running and he had been looking, because looking was what he did, and he had seen the female one go down on the floor and cover her head while the thunder was still happening.

That was not the shape a person makes when they are doing a thing on purpose.

He did not have any way to say that to a ring of sixty-two people around a fire. He did not have the words for *on purpose*. He was not sure the village had them.

So he pointed at his mother's mark on the wall, and then he held his hand out flat, palm up, which meant *this is owed*, which is a thing you say about a fishing debt or a share of a harvest.

And then, because it was the only other thing he had, he made the sign for *I will*, which is two fingers walking forward, and there was no sign in the world for the rest of it.

Rul looked at him for a while.

"He's fifteen," somebody said from the back.

"He is," said Kesh-ka, and put her sticks under her and got up, which took a long time, and nobody helped her because you did not. She looked at the wall, at the four hundred years, at the nineteen. "And he came home along the water pipe, which is forbidden, and he did not fall off it."

She turned around.

"We go down," she said. "That is decided. I am old and I will not spend the end of my life arguing with a boy." She looked at Chit-tat-to with her small black eyes. "Go up, then. Go and get what is owed. And when you have it, come and find where we have gone, and do not be surprised if it takes you a while, because we will be hiding properly."

---

He left before light, with his mother's basket and a fishing line that had been Ptik's and no plan of any kind.

At the top of the drop-shaft, where the root tunnel opened onto the surface and the cold came in, the picture-screen that had been following him for two days appeared again in front of his eyes.

It had been showing him the same thing since the room in the deep. A picture of the tall male with the thunder-stick. Under it, a mark he did not know, which he had decided meant *the one who did it*.

Now the picture had changed.

It showed a set of scales, the kind the traders used, the pan on one side heavy with small shapes he understood to be his dead, and the other pan empty.

And under the picture, for the first time in his life, there was a WORD.

Not a picture. A word, a real one, the kind the tall folk used, six marks long, sitting there in the air where he could not fail to see it.

Chit-tat-to sat down on the cold stone at the mouth of the tunnel with his mother's basket on his back and looked at it for a long time.

He did not know what it said.
