## CHAPTER 9: THE GOD OF WHAT REMAINS

The raven landed on the terrace rail with a sound like a dropped anvil, and nobody moved to meet it, because nobody had slept.

They had sat on the terrace all night with the plain going dark and then grey below them, four people and a fifth's worth of silence. Miss Hue had kept the morningstar across her knees. Mei had sat with her back to the mountain and her face to the drop. Cuong had not sat at all. He had stood at the rail from the moment they came out of the door until the sky changed, and Diep had watched him not sleep and had not been able to think of one thing to say that would be worth the air.

She had tried once, near the middle of the night.

"He went back under," she said. "You told him to clear. He was clear."

"I know what he did."

"I'm saying it wasn't"

"I know what it wasn't." He had not turned around. "Go to sleep, Diep."

She had not gone to sleep. Now the bird was on the rail, black and enormous, folding wings that made a noise like sails, and Cuong looked at it the way he had looked at the men in the market, and Diep understood that whatever came out of that bird was going to have to get past her brother first.

It came apart, and a man stood in its place.

Wolf fur. One eye. A spear that grounded on the stone with a crack Diep felt in her back teeth.

The eye went over the four of them, counted, and stopped where the fifth should have been.

"The loud one," Odin said.

"Duy," Cuong said. "Officer Duy. He held a gate."

"I know what he held. I watched." The eye came to rest on Cuong, and on the judgments over Cuong's head, and it read them without hurry, the way everyone on this world would now read him before they read his face. "You are expensive, policeman. Nineteen and five and eleven findings, and my favor at less than nothing, and one of my five spent on a door. Tell me why I should keep feeding this."

Diep saw what the safe answer was. She saw her brother see it too.

Duy had been two days in the ground.

"Because your quest got them killed," Cuong said.

The wind moved in the fur at the god's shoulders and nothing else moved anywhere.

"You posted a rescue to five people who had been murdered that morning. Into a dark room. No words on what was coming, no words on who they were, no words at all except save them, and a clock." His voice had gone into the flat register, the one from the radio, and it was shaking under the flatness. "And when it went wrong, your System wrote it down in nobody's favor but its own. So keep feeding it or don't. But you watched. You said so. You watched, and you let five terrified people find out what those cells were the hard way, and where I am from we have a word for an officer who does that with a warrant."

Miss Hue said, very softly, in Vietnamese, "Careful, boy."

Odin considered him.

It went on a long time. Somewhere below the terrace, birds Diep had no names for were saying things to each other in a forest older than her species. Mei had turned around. Diep found that she had put herself half a step in front of her brother without deciding to, and that her hands were on the hilts of the knives, and that this was almost certainly the stupidest place a person could stand.

"There he is," Odin said at last. Not to Cuong. To the air, or to the bird that was somewhere inside him. "The one who stands up. The bird said so and I doubted."

He turned his back on them, which Diep understood was its own kind of answer, and set the spear toward the mountain's shoulder.

"Sit," he said. "All of you. You have questions and I have a morning, and you will get the morning, because I owe the loud one that much and I pay what I owe."

---

They sat on the terrace stones in a half ring, and the god sat with them, and this was somehow the strangest thing that had happened yet.

He drew in the dust with the butt of the spear.

"A world," he said, and made a circle. "Yours. Eight thousand million of you, and a year, less a little, before the water finds the towns. You know this. What you do not know is that nobody is coming to stop the water." He looked up. "There is no plan to stop the water. Write that down somewhere in you."

"Then what are we for," Diep said.

"You are for the ledger." He drew a second circle, touching the first. "This world. Atlantis, you call it, which is a name from a story your people tell about a place that sank, and you are not wrong, only early." He divided the second circle with seven strokes. "Seven regions. Old, tired, half empty. Eleven pantheons quarreling over the scraps like heirs at a reading. And into this, a court."

He tapped the seam between the circles.

"The court says: a people that can defend a claim keeps it. A people that cannot, does not. It does not say this to be cruel. It says it because it was built by creatures who believed with their whole hearts that this was justice, and they built well, and they are gone, and it is still here." He sat back. "So your world's nations, all of them, every flag your maps have, hold claims here now. Ground. Real ground, this ground, portioned out by the court to be won or lost. And when your water comes, the people of a nation that holds its claim have somewhere to go."

Nobody said anything.

"And the ones that don't," Cuong said.

"Don't."

Diep looked at the dust. Two circles. Seven strokes. A year.

"How many," she said. "How many Vietnamese fit on whatever Vietnam is here."

Odin looked at her with something that was almost approval.

"That," he said, "is the first question anybody in five teams has asked me that a champion should ask. The answer is: it depends on what you hold when the water comes. Hold a valley, a valley's worth. Hold a province, a province. Hold nothing"

"Hold nothing and nobody comes."

"Nobody comes."

Miss Hue spoke for the first time since her warning. "And the other four teams. The ones you briefed before us."

"Dead, mostly. Or gone in a way that is worse than dead, and I will explain that another morning, when you are stronger and it will not break you." He said it without cruelty, the way he said everything. "I choose the ones who die together. It is the only thing I have found that predicts anything. Five people who died in one place on one morning have something between them that I cannot manufacture, and I have stopped trying."

Diep did not look at Mei. She felt the not-looking like a hand on the back of her neck.

"She stabbed me," Diep said. "That's what was between us. You picked her because of it."

"I picked her because of it," Odin agreed. "A knife counts as together. It counts more than most things." He stood, and the spear came up with him. "You want to hate me for that, girl, you get in line behind your brother. Now. The classing hall is at Threshold, two days up the pass, and you will want what it gives you, because the court has posted Vietnam's claim, and the claim will not wait for your grief."

He paused, and did not turn around.

"Nothing waits for grief. That is not the court. That is everything."

---

They gathered their little gear.

At the door of the keeping place, Cuong stopped and looked back once, down the dark corridor toward a doorless cell, and Diep stood beside him and did not hurry him.

"He wanted to take his mother to Da Lat," Cuong said. "Eleven months of saving. He told everyone. He told the spring roll auntie."

"I know."

"I'm going to have to be the one who tells her. When we. If there's a when." He shook his head once, hard, the way he did when he was refusing a thought. "I don't know how you tell a woman her son died holding a gate for people she's never met on a world that isn't there."

"You say it the way you just said it," Diep said. "You say the gate."

He looked at her.

"You say he held a gate. That's the whole thing. She'll know what that means about him, because she made him."

Cuong stood a moment longer. Then he pulled the door of the keeping place shut on the dark, and it swung to on hinges that no human had hung, and he put his hand flat on the wood.

"Held a gate," he said.

Odin's voice came from up the road without kindness or cruelty, a report from an old front.

"He died holding a gate against a warden twice his weight, laughing," the god said. "In nine thousand years I have furnished halls with less. Now walk."

They walked.

The road went up between broken pillars toward a pass that had been cut by somebody who was not afraid of mountains, and Diep took the front of it, because someone had to, and because the word for what she was had a claim posted against it, and because there was no version of this morning where her brother should have to walk in front.
