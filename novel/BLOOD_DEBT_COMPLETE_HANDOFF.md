# Blood Debt / The Merge: Universe Writing Skill

This skill governs HOW to write in this universe. The story bible governs WHAT is true.

**Before drafting or auditing any prose in this universe, also read `voice_models.md` in this skill folder: the sourced technical spec for the Griffin and Brooks registers, including verified craft moves and banned anti-patterns.**

## Rule zero: the bible is the source of truth
The canonical story bible and chapter drafts are files Tony keeps and re-uploads. This skill never duplicates continuity facts (ages, timelines, locked plot decisions), because facts change and duplication creates drift.
- If the bible is in context: audit all output against it, especially the Continuity Anchors (top of Section 7) and Planted Payoffs (Section 11).
- If the bible is NOT in context and the task involves canon (chapters, character work, plot design): ask Tony to upload it before drafting. Style-only tasks (a sample System message, a voice exercise) may proceed on this skill alone, flagged as unaudited.

## The three voice registers
1. **The Auditor (System records and notifications):** dry, precise, procedurally cruel, involuntarily humane. Never exclaims. Paired since the two-voice doctrine with **the Magistrate** (presiding voice of contested proceedings: TV-court-judge energy, Judge Judy and Judge Mathis, self-aggrandizing, interrupting, usually technically correct; no sponsors, ratings, or viewer economy, ever). **The thesis both voices serve: the System is a court, not a game show. Suffering as procedure, not content. It is hateable because it processes pain correctly, and correctness is not justice. Origin canon: built by the vanished Authors to end injustice, running their moral code without mercy, because they held mercy to be inequality before the law.** Judgment-and-precedent mechanics are binding: notifications rule rather than celebrate; notable awards cite prior recipients and their fates ("Outcomes are not guaranteed."), the series' legend engine; cultural-mismatch buffs descend from Author values (Honest Feet, Clean Hands, Contempt of Court, Salt Oath), discovered in play, never info-dumped; and failures, deaths, and debts arrive in silence and fine print, so the reader learns to fear the quiet screens. Calibration lines: "This entry is permanent. So are they." / "Unlocked is not the same as forgiven." / "It did not help. They rarely do."
2. **Griffin register (human chapters).** Competent professionals understated about horrifying things, written the way Griffin actually writes: procedure explained with patient, dry precision. Griffin walks the reader through the stance, the weapon, the protocol; he never clips into insider shorthand. The explanation carries the authority, so clarity IS the register and jargon is never exempt from self-glossing. Humor from procedure, rank friction, gallows economy. Banter is earned by competence. "Arithmetic" is Cuong's word only; other characters weigh, price, or pay.
3. **Brooks register (Chit-tat-to chapters).** Warmth, wonder, fable-cadence, grief underneath. His language complexity tracks his sentience stat; his growth in words IS his leveling.
Calibration reference for any uncertain scene: Miss Hue. Dry authority, patience as a weapon, jokes that are also verdicts, never a sweet-old-lady joke.

## THE FINAL SENTENCE LAW (highest-priority check; violated more than any other rule)
The last sentence of every paragraph is the highest-risk position in the draft, because that is where the instinct to decorate fires. After drafting ANY paragraph, read its final sentence alone and ask: is this a fact, or an ornament? Ornaments include personification ("the dark took the word and handed it to all of them"), retrospective softening ("that was the worst part of it, later"), narrated interiority ("the two facts sat side by side in his head without connecting"), aphorism ("pain was information"), and summarized emotion ("she was screaming something about the corn"). All of these die. The paragraph ends on the last fact and stops. If the ending feels flat, that is correct; flat is the target. Facts do not need help, and the reader supplies every feeling worth having.

## Hard bans (absolute, from Tony's standing rules)
- No em dashes or en dashes, anywhere, including System messages.
- **No narrated interiority.** Never explain a character's mental process, never name what they realize or understand or recognize. Show the body: what it does, what it fails to do, what it holds. The reader infers the mind. Banned specimens: "the two facts sat side by side in his head without connecting," "Diep understood, with a clarity that would stay with her," "she recognized it from tournament mornings."
- **No summarized speech in high emotion.** If a character is screaming, write what they scream. Banned specimen: "she was screaming something about the corn, about forty people."
- **Nobody is hardened before the page hardens them.** Courage is performed by frightened people; wounds hurt, fear shows, composure is earned scene by scene. Banned specimen: a nineteen-year-old athlete "reorganizing for round two" minutes after being stabbed.
- No rhetorical questions in narration (dialogue questions are fine).
- No "not X but Y" constructions. No dramatic colons. No rule-of-three flourishes.
- Minimal adjectives. Plain language legible to a cold reader.
- No character introduced via height/weight/measurements; physicality through action only.
- No reference-joke humor. No head-hopping: POV changes at chapter breaks only.
- No lore delivered as documents the characters read; worldbuilding is discovered through scenes.
- No notification spam: never repeat a notification format twice in one chapter.

## Craft rules (each earned from a Tony correction; binding)
1. **First-line rule.** Every chapter, and every major POV or scene shift, opens on motion, sensation, or threat. Never a wry framing observation. Maximum force on the first line of the book.
2. **Banter clarity.** A joke must land on first read. If decoding requires re-reading the prior line, rewrite it explicit or cut it.
3. **No unearned weight, no image-riddles.** Gravitas only when the scene has banked it. If a sensory observation needs decoding, write the concrete version of what it reaches for, or cut it.
4. **Self-glossing terms.** Vietnamese terms are an asset and every one must carry its own gloss in context on first use ("the Bien Dong, the wide sea the world's maps call the South China"). Same for technical/nautical/martial jargon. Register is never an exemption: procedural, military, and police vocabulary self-glosses like all other jargon.
5. **The "why would he think that" test.** Every perception, deduction, and reaction must have its cause on the page. Circular or unmotivated character knowledge dies in draft.
6. **Read-aloud check.** The book is audio-first. Sweep every chapter for doubled words in close proximity, stacked trailing clauses, and passives.
7. **Dramatize, never report.** Cool content is never summarized ("she had hunted elk with a bow in Mongolia, and she was not above mentioning it" is the banned form). Worth the reader's time means on the page as scene: dialogue, action, texture. Oft-told family stories are delivered as call-and-response, characters completing each other's sentences and correcting details, showing intimacy while the content lands. Not worth dramatizing means cut entirely. There is no middle register.
8. **Vietnamese term convention.** Names romanize without diacritics (Diep, Cuong, Phu Nhuan). Cultural terms italicize with full diacritics on first use, glossed as natural apposition (tất niên, lô đề), never as encyclopedia entries.
9. **Cornered confessions.** Characters never volunteer apologies, confessions, or backstory unprompted; they get cornered into them by other characters, circumstances, or evidence. Volunteered exposition in dialogue is the "as you know" disease.
10. **First-reference check.** After any cut or structural edit, verify every named character's first remaining appearance still carries its identifying context, so trims never orphan an introduction.
11. **Agency stays with people.** Tools, weapons, body parts, and scenery do not act ("the blade took the half beat," "mountains took the first sun" are the banned forms). The person acts, with or through the thing. When a personification seems worth keeping, the default is still plain: Tony grants exceptions, the model never argues for its own. Do not defend flourishes; convert them and present the plain version.

## Screen discipline (System messages and stats)
1. Maximum one full status screen per chapter, only when a number changes the scene.
2. Notifications are dialogue from the Auditor, never database output. Each must do character work or comedy work.
3. Skill flavor text is a punchline budget: one per chapter.
4. All System text written to be performed aloud; read it flat, and if it is not funny or chilling, rewrite.
5. Numbers matter narratively or not at all.
6. **Achievements pay on the page**: concrete stats/skills/items at issue. "To be disclosed" is banned. Mystery lives in implications, never hidden mechanics. Unique grades state their uniqueness ("This will never be awarded again.").

## Chapter spec (Royal Road serial)
- Target ~2,500 words. Every chapter ends on a pull: question, threat, or cost.
- Hook obligations live in the bible outline (Section 10); consult before structuring.
- Permadeath is absolute. Never write a resurrection, rewind, or dream-out. Gods CAN resurrect at ruinous cost; that cost is plot, not an exit.

## Sensitive-content rules (binding)
- Tuan's institutional abuse: never depicted in scene, no graphic description anywhere in the series; implication, aftermath, testimony, and System records only (also a hard Royal Road and Amazon platform requirement).
- Both/and discipline for the pathologized-seer material: the visions AND the damage are real; medication was suppression AND survival; vindication is partial and late; no crazy-equals-violent; Mười is never laundered (exploiter AND the only adult who valued Tuan, both on the page).

## Pre-delivery audit (run before handing Tony any chapter or canon content)

**STAGE ZERO, THE SIMULATION PASS (runs before the prose rules, scene by scene):** rebuild each scene as a world-state and interrogate every action against it. For every scene, inventory: WHO is present and why each person is here today at this hour; WHAT each character knows, and how they learned it; TRUST, would X actually permit Y (the helm test); POSSESSIONS AND CHANNELS, who owns, carries, or can contact what (the phone-number test); CLOCK AND CALENDAR, does everyone's presence fit the day, the schedule, the season (the finals test); CAPABILITY, is each feat earned by established training and state (the navy-seal test, the wit-after-waking test). Any action that fails its interrogation dies or gets its cause put on the page. This pass exists because prose-rule checking cannot catch world-logic failures, and because the model trusts scenes it generated; interrogate them as if a hostile reader wrote them.

**STAGE ONE, THE INFORMATION PASS (runs after simulation, before the prose rules):** audit every fact's delivery. (a) Strongest-carrier rule: dialogue, action, objects, and System screens outrank narration; narration is the carrier of last resort, used only when no scene element can carry the fact. (b) No narrator scoops: never pre-state what a built scene element delivers later; the payoff owns the reveal (the broken-dream failure: narration announced "the final she had won became a final she was losing" when Cuong's "You already beat Trang" and the trophy glance were built to deliver it). (c) Reveal-order interrogation: for each fact, ask whether it lands harder earlier or later, and whether reader-knowledge and character-knowledge diverge on purpose or by accident. (d) Slot audit: anonymous roles ("her opponent") are wasted slots when a named element could occupy them and pay off elsewhere (the Trang failure).

1. Continuity: every age, date, place, and number checked against the bible's Continuity Anchors. Known error classes: stale ages, dead numbers from earlier drafts, district drift, timeline math.
2. Hard bans sweep, including the em dash and rule-of-three scans.
3. Craft rules 1 through 6, chapter by chapter, including first lines at every scene shift.
4. Screen discipline on every System message.
5. Planted Payoffs: nothing promised gets contradicted; new promises get logged to the bible.
6. Motif ownership: check the bible's motif registry (arithmetic is Cuong's).
7. **Design-change propagation:** whenever the bible changes a character or system, sweep ALL existing prose featuring them for stale characterization, not just the System messages. A redesigned character wearing old-draft reactions is a continuity error even when every fact is technically correct.
8. **Blast-radius check, per edit, not per audit:** after ANY structural edit (move, cut, reorder), re-read the paragraphs on both sides of every affected site before responding, tracing what each assumes (references, antecedents, chronology, planted setups). Orphans are created at edit time and must be caught at edit time; waiting for a batch audit means the author catches them first, which is a process failure.
When delivering, state that the audit ran and list anything it caught. "Checked" means this happened.

- He wants honest pushback with receipts, a recommendation rather than unranked options, and fast decisions honored once made.

# Voice Models Reference: Griffin and Brooks
## Load this file whenever drafting or auditing register in Blood Debt / The Merge.

This is a technical style spec, not a quote anthology. Copyright limits verbatim excerpts, so the models are described as craft moves, sourced from published analyses and from Tony's direct reading of both authors. Tony's reading outranks this file; when he corrects it, the file updates.

---

## W.E.B. GRIFFIN (human-chapters register)

**The verified core:** Griffin's military fiction is famous for how little of it is combat. His Brotherhood of War series is noted specifically for the attention it does NOT devote to battle; reader analyses converge on the same point, that his books are about the behind-the-scenes work, the process, logistics, politics, and institutional life that surround the fighting.

**Craft moves (write these):**
1. **Procedure explained at point of use.** When a character does something trained, the page walks through it: the stance described foot by foot, the weapon named, gripped, and operated in sequence, the protocol laid out step by step. Tony's touchstone paraphrase of the mode: "He assumed a boxer's firing stance, one foot in front of the other, both hands gripping the Beretta, leaning forward." The explanation IS the authority. A reader who knows nothing about guns or radios finishes the paragraph knowing exactly what happened and why it was competent.
2. **Institutional texture carried precisely.** Ranks, forms of address, chains of command, paperwork, who may say what to whom. Characters are located in a system and the system is rendered accurately.
3. **Dialogue does the heavy lifting.** Long, competent conversations between professionals; information moves through talk, and character shows in who defers, who needles, who is careful.
4. **Understatement as register.** Horrifying things described in the flat voice of people whose job includes them. The emotion is in the restraint, never in the adjectives.
5. **Dry humor through official language.** Jokes arrive deadpan inside procedure, bureaucratese, and rank friction, never as gags.
6. **Repetition of institutional detail as comfort.** Griffin re-explains gear and protocol across books; readers tease him for it and read on. The lesson for us: never fear explaining, fear failing to.

**Anti-patterns (Griffin is NOT this; banned under his name):**
- Clipped insider shorthand ("front sight, press"). That is a different thriller tradition entirely.
- Unglossed jargon as flavor. Griffin glosses by instinct; the gloss is the flavor.
- Macho terseness, fragments-as-toughness, competence signaled by withholding explanation.

**Correction on record:** an earlier draft of this project defended clipped jargon as "Griffin register." Tony, who has read him, caught it; sources confirmed Tony. Register is never a jargon exemption.

---

## TERRY BROOKS (Chit-tat-to-chapters register)

**The verified core:** classic quest fantasy with measured, evocative prose (Brooks cites Faulkner as a formative influence on his style; he wrote his senior thesis on Faulkner while beginning The Sword of Shannara). Reviewers consistently credit warmth, world-building, memorable characters, and storytelling earnestness; the books are widely described as big-hearted rather than ironic.

**Craft moves (write these):**
1. **Earnestness without apology.** Wonder, loyalty, and courage are presented straight. The narration believes in the quest.
2. **Ordinary protagonists.** Leads are reluctant, small, underestimated people onto whom great weight falls; growth is the plot.
3. **Landscape as mood.** Terrain and weather are rendered with care and carry the emotional register of the chapter.
4. **Fellowship as engine.** Bonds between companions, formed on the road, are the story's load-bearing structure.
5. **Measured pacing.** Scenes breathe; travel, camps, and quiet conversations are given room, and dread builds slowly.
6. **Clear moral stakes.** Evil is real, choices matter, and the cost of courage is honestly paid.

**Anti-patterns (Brooks is NOT this):**
- Irony poisoning, winking narration, grimdark flippancy.
- Rushed wonder; a marvel summarized instead of walked through.
- Cynical protagonists whose competence replaces growth.

---

## APPLICATION IN BLOOD DEBT

- Human chapters take Griffin: explained procedure, institutional precision, understatement, deadpan-official humor. The self-glossing rule and this register are the same instinct.
- Chit-tat-to chapters take Brooks: earnest wonder, fellowship, landscape, growth, with grief underneath.
- The Auditor is neither; it is its own register (see SKILL.md) and must not drift toward either model.
- Calibration test when unsure: would this line survive in a Griffin procedural (does it explain, precisely, dryly)? Would this line survive in a Brooks quest chapter (does it believe in itself)?

## 3. LOGLINE AND PITCH

**Logline:** When a Ho Chi Minh City market massacre kills her whole world, a 19-year-old martial artist is recruited by desperate gods to fight for humanity's survival on a refugee supercontinent about to crash into the Pacific. Her first act as Earth's champion is an atrocity. Her only way forward is to repay it, alongside the last survivor of the people she helped kill.

**The two-protagonist pitch:** Diep climbs the ladder every LitRPG reader knows: levels, classes, a tower, a countdown. Chit-tat-to climbs a ladder no one has written: his species is rated "barely sentient" by the System itself, his people read their own status screens as crude pictures, and he is the first Ratkin whose screen begins rendering in words. Diep is fighting to save a world. Chit-tat-to is fighting to be counted as a person. The stories braid.

**The DCC-lesson checklist this design satisfies:**
- Co-lead as strong as the protagonist (Chit-tat-to = the Donut slot, played for pathos-first instead of vanity-first)
- System with personality (see Section 6)
- Dark comedy amid real violence, stakes that never reset
- Found family: a party held together by guilt, debt, and stubborn love
- Audio-first design: every System message written to be performed

## 4. WORLD RULES

### 4.1 The Situation
Two thousand years ago, the being Earth knows as Adonai sealed Earth's universe into a pocket dimension to protect humanity from the multiverse. Pocket universes decay. Earth's has roughly one year left. When it fails, Earth reintegrates: the Sakti (ambient life-force that powers magic and the System) floods the planet, animals ascend toward sentience, and every sentient being gets a status screen. Most of humanity is projected to die in the transition. This projection is delivered by the System in the tone of an actuary.

Atlantis is the other pocket-dimension experiment: a 136-million-square-kilometer circular supercontinent built a millennium ago by Elven and Dwarven gods as a refugee camp for dying worlds. Seven wedge-shaped regions, one per species-group: Humans (kingdom of Leath, arrived 100 years ago), Elves, Dwarves, Goblinkind, Nijiit (feline), Gnomes, and the shared Wylac/Kilar ocean region. Five hundred years of near-constant border war. At the center: an 8-million-square-kilometer no-man's land of the worst monsters of every world, and at its heart, the Tower.

When Earth's pocket fails, Atlantis reintegrates too, surfacing in the middle of the Pacific. Earth gets magic, monsters, AND five billion armed refugees with a millennium of grudges, on the same day.

**Lore delivery rule: nothing above is ever delivered as a document.** It is discovered: a border skirmish witnessed, a Leath politician's speech, a Ratkin pictogram history-wall, a drunk dwarf's complaint. The draft's read-along infodump is banned.

### 4.2 The Champion Program
Adonai and Odin (and analogous patron gods worldwide; Vietnam's are the Four Immortals) recruit the newly and violently dead. The offer: a second life as a Champion or Companion on Atlantis. Mission: establish a human foothold, ally with at least one major faction, and climb the Tower to win resources, legitimacy, and power before integration day.

- **Champions** hold the quest, make binding decisions, gain faster progression, and bear the penalties. One primary Champion per national slot.
- **Companions** are bound to a Champion. Slower base progression, but they share party experience and can hold roles Champions cannot (a Companion can betray no one; the System enforces it, which is its own horror).
- Prior champion cohorts have gone missing, gone native, or gone dark. The Italians (special forces, perfect scores) vanished two years ago. The Burundi/Malawi group went into Leath politics. This is a mystery thread, not backstory.

### 4.3 Death and the Favor Economy
- Death is permanent for champions, companions, and natives alike.
- Gods can resurrect, at catastrophic cost to the power reserves keeping Earth's pocket stable. Every resurrection granted shortens Earth's countdown. The gods will almost never do it, and when one finally does (late series), it should bankrupt something.
- **Divine Favor** is the currency between mortals and patrons: earned by completing quests, honoring a patron's values, and impressive deeds; spent on blessings, information, emergency intervention, and appeals. Favor debts (like the party's -100 with Odin) function as moral liens. The Blood Debt questline (Section 8) is how the party works theirs off.

### 4.4 The System
- Universal interface: status, skills, quests, party mechanics, notifications. **Canonical framing: the System calls a status screen an "account," and defines it as a record of your body, your deeds, and your debts.** This framing debuts in the cold open and recurs; it ties the interface to the ledger motif (Blood Debt, favor liens, the attrition ledger) that runs through the whole series.
- Attributes: Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Intelligence, Wisdom, Luck. HP = 10x CON. MP = 5x INT.
- Skills: Novice (1-20), Apprentice (20-40), Journeyman, Expert, Master. Tier-ups grant a modifier or linked skill.
- Classes are earned through Trials, ranked Common / Rare / Epic / Legendary.
- Earth skills translate (Vovinam, police training, lockpicking, cooking). This is Vietnam's edge and the book's flavor.
- Firearms are "Forbidden" grade: outside multiverse physics, devastating, finite. Ammunition does not respawn. The guns are a melting ice cube, and the book treats each magazine as a strategic resource until they're gone (target: dry by end of Act 2, forcing full conversion to System-native combat).
- **Sentience is a stat the System tracks.** Ratkin sit at the threshold. This is the co-lead's entire arc made mechanical.

### 4.5 The Partition (replaces the Tower)
When Earth integrates, every faction in the multiverse wants a piece of a defenseless Eden. The gods' leverage bought one concession: adjudication before invasion. Earth's territory is divided into **claims** (its ~195 nations plus the 7 regions of Atlantis itself), each of which can be formally contested before integration day.

- **Contests happen in simulacra.** The Adjudication Grounds at the center of the no-man's land instantiate each contested region as a rendered zone, populated with the exact forces slated to enter it on integration day. Earth's champions defend copies of their homelands.
- **Win:** the region's integration terms are written in humanity's favor (sanctuaries, protective Sakti infrastructure, gentler beast ascension, population blessings). **Lose:** the claim transfers to the winner, and the horde assigned to that region is reinforced and pre-positioned. The whole map cashes out at once on integration day.
- **The world map is the scoreboard.** Claims resolve green or red across the series, in scheduled adjudication waves (campaign seasons). Readers track the map; the map is the serial engine.
- **Compression:** most of Earth's champion teams are dead, missing, or gone native. Undefended claims forfeit unless another nation's team stands proxy. Whose homeland do you defend when you cannot defend them all: the series' central strategic question and the Blood Debt engine at planetary scale.
- **Contest format is MMO battleground design**, not dungeon-crawl floors: multi-phase objectives (capture and hold, escort, siege) with a **persistent attrition ledger**: damage, deaths, and resource spend carry across phases, no resets. Zone design adapts the Ring Space drifting-objective framework and Broken Clock boss timing where fitting.
- **The discarded races (Ratkin among them) have no claim standing anywhere, by law.** Chit-tat-to's series goal made concrete: win standing first, then win a region. The villain space: whoever profits from the discarded staying discarded.
- Rival claimants include other worlds' champion cohorts, Atlantis factions, and claims-broker consortia fielding hired or enslaved proxies (Mei's pit thread plugs in here).

Selection: each nation confirms one Champion of standing. The count of confirmed defenders at series start is 201, and it is shrinking.

### 4.6 The Wall's Ledger (Adonai's debts; a story-generation engine)
Canon principle: the seal Adonai built around Earth is the root system under the series' systemic problems. When a condition needs a cause, check the wall first.
1. **The lost century** (canon, cold open): worlds receive ~100 years of integration notice; Earth's was spent hidden. "He is sorry."
2. **The leak** (canon, Section 7.8): the decaying wall seeped Sakti for roughly two decades; sensitives worldwide were damaged and gifted by the same exposure.
3. **The starved pantheons:** the wall throttled divine connection to Earth for two millennia. Miracles stopped and gods went silent because they were cut off; Earth's pantheons nearly faded. Odin's bitterness and the gods' visible fear are wall-caused. The divine grudge engine: Adonai's brother gods paid for his choice.
4. **The queue at the Arch:** the wall throttled soul processing; Earth's dead wait in a line generations deep. Locked thread: Miss Hue's husband, dead ~30 years, may still be in that line. Her patron is the god whose wall built the queue. Planted in Ch 3.
5. **Level 1, almost everyone:** two millennia without Sakti left humanity mechanically stunted relative to every other integrating world. The wall made them weak the day it was built to keep them safe.
6. **The compressed ascension:** Earth's fauna ascends in a spike rather than a curve because ascension was dammed for two thousand years.
7. **The entangled pockets:** the Elven and Dwarven gods modeled Atlantis's pocket partly on Adonai's (draft canon, now locked as consequence). The pockets are entangled; his wall's decay destabilized theirs; Atlantis crashing into Earth's Pacific, specifically, flows downhill from his choice.
8. **The spent war chest:** maintaining the wall drained the gods' reserves. This is why resurrection is ruinous, intervention is rationed, and the champion program is five dead people from Phu Nhuan instead of an army.

**The limit (binding):** the wall explains systemic conditions, never personal evil. Villains choose: the claims-brokers, Nam Cam's network, and the orderly are chosen evils, not wall-caused. Monocausality flattens a series; Adonai's guilt is the theology, not the excuse.
**Endgame note (not locked):** the god who built the wall paying the series' one ruinous resurrection is a candidate for the final entry in his ledger. Decide much later.

## 5. STAT SCREEN DISCIPLINE (COMMERCIAL RULE SET)

1. Maximum one full status screen per chapter, and only when a number changes the scene.
2. Notifications are dialogue from a character (the System), never database output. If a notification isn't doing character work or comedy work, cut it.
3. Never repeat a notification format twice in one chapter.
4. Skill flavor text is a punchline budget: one per chapter.
5. All System text written to be performed aloud (audio-first). Read every message in a flat bureaucrat voice; if it isn't funny or chilling, rewrite.
6. Numbers matter narratively or not at all. "Diep's health dropped below a third" beats "Diep took 47 damage" unless 47 is the joke.
7. **Achievements pay on the page.** Every achievement grants visible, concrete rewards at the moment of issue: stat awards, and where earned, unique skills, powers, or items. The System never withholds reward specifications ("to be disclosed" is banned); the Auditor discloses with precision, and its menace lives in HOW it discloses, never in hidden mechanics. Mystery belongs to implications ("This is a gift, and is also not"), not to concealed numbers. Unique-grade achievements state their uniqueness explicitly ("This will never be awarded again"), because scarcity shown beats scarcity implied.

## 6. THE SYSTEM'S VOICES (TWO, BY DESIGN)

The comedy engine, split to avoid DCC's lane while delivering the genre's fanfare.

**Voice one: THE AUDITOR (records, notifications, screens).** A vast, ancient, rule-bound bureaucracy with a buried conscience. Dry, precise, procedurally cruel, occasionally and involuntarily humane. Never uses exclamation points. Never lies, but chooses disclosures, and the choices are characterization. Mercy must be deniable. Its menace lives in HOW it discloses, never in hidden mechanics.

**Voice two: THE MAGISTRATE (presiding voice of contested proceedings; working name).** Not a game-show host and not a crier: a TV-court judge at multiverse scale, Judge Judy and Judge Mathis energy, the judiciary as personality. Self-aggrandizing, interrupting, moralizing from a bench nine millennia deep, openly contemptuous of litigants, and, infuriatingly, usually technically correct. Presides over claim contests at the Adjudication Grounds (debuts Act 3); the broadcasts exist because rulings require witnesses, and the Magistrate performs justice the way TV judges do, as a mockery of what justice is supposed to look like that never once breaks procedure. Sample register: "Nine millennia on this bench and champions still believe grief is an argument." / "This court has outlived four pantheons who assumed its patience was negotiable." / "I know what you did. You know what you did. We will not waste the ledger's time pretending otherwise." **Hard limits (anti-DCC): no sponsors, no ratings, no viewer economy. Nobody profits from watching, and that is somehow worse.** Friction with the Auditor is court-flavored: the clerk resents the bench's editorializing ("The presiding remarks are not the System's. The record reflects that.").

**JUDGMENT AND PRECEDENT MECHANICS (binding; replaces fanfare):** notifications do not celebrate, they RULE. Every award is a finding; significant awards carry editorial weight and, above all, PRECEDENT. Notable achievements cite prior recipients and their subsequent fates ("Most recent prior recipient: Szel of Vekh-Amun, subsequently First Navigator of the Unbroken Fleet. Outcomes are not guaranteed."), which is the series' legend-building engine, the Anarchist's Cookbook slot: recurring legendary names accrete across citations, prior recipients become aspirational or cautionary lore, and readers learn the multiverse through boilerplate. "Outcomes are not guaranteed" is the canonical disclaimer. Failures, deaths, and debts still arrive in silence and fine print, no editorial, no precedent, and the reader learns to fear the quiet screens.

**OFFICES (generalization of the precedent engine):** Unique-grade titles carry duties as well as buffs; the award is also an appointment. Title-holders across worlds form living registries the court can call upon (the 1,406 First Witnesses are a witness registry; the vindicated-disbelieved staff the Unheard Docket, Section 7.8). Precedent citations are therefore not trivia but a NETWORK: prior recipients are colleagues, predecessors, and future allies or problems, and the legends the citations build can walk onstage.

**ORIGIN CANON (known premise; nature unanswered until series-late):** an ancient civilization, working name THE AUTHORS, set out to remove injustice from the galaxy and built the System as its enforcement entity. It applies justice through THEIR moral code, complete, consistent, and merciless, because the Authors held mercy to be favoritism, an inequality before the law. The Authors are gone; the code runs on. Everything hateable about the System is someone's sincere idea of fairness, which is the thesis with its origin attached.

**CULTURAL MISMATCH MECHANICS (the Carl's-boxers slot; Author values fossilized as rules):** absurd buffs and debuffs descend from the Authors' moral code, and nobody alive knows why. Canon starters: **Honest Feet** (the Authors held that the honest walk unshod; barefoot acts of testimony, oath, and combat gain a truth-and-honor bonus, which Vovinam-trained barefoot fighters trigger constantly by accident, and which the party discovers before understanding). **Clean Hands** (washing before a petition grants a small bonus to appeals; the doctrine is literal). **Contempt of Court** (dismissing or interrupting a System screen before it finishes rendering incurs a micro-fine: "Reading time is court time."). **Salt Oath** (contracts sworn over shared salt bind at a higher grade; Author hospitality law, never explained). Add sparingly, one per arc at most, always discovered in play, never info-dumped.

**THE ARGUMENT (why we hate our System; the book's thesis, distinct from DCC):** DCC's engine is suffering as content. Ours is suffering as PROCEDURE. The System is a court that is never wrong and never just: perfect records, zero mercy. Grief is filed under aggravating factors, a massacre becomes a title, a homeland becomes a claim number, and the debts you owe are calculated to the soul while the debts owed to YOU are unenforceable, because standing is granted, not possessed, and the discarded have none. Vietnam recognizes this machine, because colonization arrived as surveyors and land courts before it arrived as soldiers, and the System is the surveyor of the multiverse. Every System beat in the book serves this argument: the Auditor is the honest clerk of an obscene jurisdiction, the Herald is its ceremony, the gods are its landlord-litigants, and Chit-tat-to's arc is the discarded suing existence for personhood. We never hate the System for enjoying our pain. We hate it because it processes our pain correctly, and correctness is not justice.

**The relationship (the engine):** one System, two voices that visibly endure each other: the clerk and the bench. The Auditor's records are exact and unadorned; the Magistrate's rulings are performances the Auditor is required to transcribe and plainly resents. Their friction is renewable comedy that no single-voiced System runs.

**Sample messages (Auditor register, canonical):**

> **Quest Failed: Save the Ratkin.**
> Cause of failure: you shot them.
> Favor with Odin: -100.
> This entry is permanent. So are they.

> Your party has been awarded 700 experience.
> Experience is awarded without regard to circumstance.
> This is not the System's favorite rule either.

> **Title Earned: Caedite Eos.**
> Awarded for the elimination of 24 hostiles.
> 19 of the 24 have been reclassified: non-combatant.
> Reclassification does not affect the award. Titles, like history, are written by the survivors.

> Skill increased: Short Blades, Level 21.

> He has been notified.

> Debuff acquired: **Blood Debt.**
> This debuff cannot be dispelled, resisted, prayed away, or outrun.
> It can only be repaid.

**Sample lines (Herald register, for the Grounds):** "SEVEN REGIONS AND HONORED GUESTS OF ELEVEN WORLDS, tell them what they're fighting for!" / "A brand new integration clause, sanctuaries INCLUDED, gods and monsters, LOOK at those terms!" / "Contestants, remember: the ledger carries, the crowd remembers, and somebody is going home green."

**Rules for both:** the Auditor never exclaims; the Herald never stops. Long game: who wrote the System, why the Auditor keeps a rule it hates, and what the Herald is under the glitter are series-scale mysteries. Do not answer in Book 1.

### 7.1 Diep Loc, 19 (aged up from 17) — Earth Lead, Champion of Vietnam
Second-year university student. Vovinam weapons champion who beat her cop brother in open competition (canon, and a permanent running joke the System participates in). Wound: her best friend put a knife in her stomach at a market stall; her city, her parents' world, and her partner-in-crime brother's partner are gone; and the first thing she did with her second life was participate in a massacre. Arc, Book 1: from little sister to commander. She did not ask to outrank Cuong; the System made her Champion and the book makes her earn it. Her weapon skills translate spectacularly; her judgment has to catch up. Voice: dry, watchful, faster to act than to speak, sharper humor than anyone expects.

**Competence rule (binding, from Tony's note):** Pre-Atlantis Diep is a sport athlete, never an operator. Her reflexes read ring-trained (fast, technical, guard up, and slightly absurd outside a ring), never tactical (no weapon staging, no threat tracking, no clearing rooms, no knife on the nightstand). She is also a 19-year-old with ordinary insecurities: new friendships matter too much, her mother's opinions land, she rehearses conversations. The hardening happens ON the page, earned through Atlantis chapter by chapter; if she reads hardened before the hardening, the arc is broken. The same rule applies in reverse to Cuong: he has real tactical training, and the contrast between her ring instincts and his procedure is a renewable source of both comedy and grief.

**The step-up arc (locked, from Tony's design):** she grew up in her brother's shadow and was catching up before the massacre, but not confidently; her anxiety dreams rewrite wins as losses. On Atlantis the court inverts everything: she holds Champion rank, he carries crippling Judgments, and if she does not step up and lead, he dies. Her leadership is born as a legal act (she signs Standing Surety for him first, before she believes in her own signature) and then must become real, decision by decision, while the whole party grows by carrying a man they love. His parallel arc: the protector learning to be protected. Her scoreboard psyche transforms across the book from fighting for points nobody keeps into keeping the only score that matters, his balance falling.

**Class: THE FOUNDER (Rare at acquisition, Legendary growth path).** Diep fights as a Vovinam weapons master; the class's unique mechanic is **Transmission**: she can formalize her art as a System-recognized School. Anyone who takes the **Disciple's Oath** enters it, and the trade runs both ways:
- At a disciple's class selection, Diep **co-authors** the outcome. She does not pick their class (agency theft flattens the cast and reviewers punish it); she unlocks options one full rank above what the disciple could reach alone, drawn from the School's technique pool plus the disciple's own history. The disciple chooses. Sworn followers get strictly better classes AND stay people.
- **Costs:** a disciple's class is School-bound; leaving the School weakens it. Every oath is a genuine decision, every departure is a story. And Diep carries command liability: her disciples' deeds and deaths land on her favor ledger. Command responsibility, mechanized (Griffin register in system form).
- **Cast consequences:** Cuong swears first; the System enjoys the rank inversion. Mei begs to swear as penance; Diep refuses to accept the oath for most of Book 1 (trust, mechanized). Chit-tat-to refuses fealty outright, because the fastest road to power runs through an oath to the people who killed his mother, and he will not kneel for it. What he negotiates instead, late in Book 1, is a **Compact between equals**, which becomes the legal instrument of Ratkin standing under the Partition.
- **Strategic layer:** under the Partition, a School can train claim defenders at scale. The Founder class is humanity's export technology. **Lineage (locked):** the class is the direct descendant of Nguyễn Lộc's founding of Vovinam under occupation, a system built so a threatened people need not borrow their defense; Miss Hue, trained in his earliest classes, is the living link, and she names the lineage when the School is founded.

Ratkin adolescent, sole survivor of the cell-block community. CONFIRMED: his mother, one of the four Elders killed and keeper of the village history wall, is among the dead. **Anti-shallow rules:** no flashback grief montages, ever. Discharge at 14, purchased with the spotless record his silence built. Present at the Phu Nhuan massacre at 15, recognizing faces from years-old visions and finding his doctrine useless at the speed of gunfire; readmitted after. Released, sent to the fishing boat of his uncle, Captain Phan Van Loi, out of the Vung Tau coast. 16 at Landfall. **Belief chronology (locked):** as a child he genuinely did not know what the seeings were and half-believed the diagnosis; the Mười years ended the doubt, because a two-year winning book is empirical proof. By 16 he knows the seeings are real. "I don't know" therefore began as truth in childhood and hardened into protective armor; the cold open renders this evolution ("somewhere in the years of saying it, it had quietly become a lie"). Never write him as lifelong-certain or lifelong-doubting; the hinge is the ward book.

**Canon (cold open):** among his seeings, one recurred from childhood, an impossible coastline he drew compulsively on intake forms and worksheets; the doctors read the drawings as fixation. Chronic insomnia is canon (he has rarely ever slept well), which is why he is awake in the bow at dawn, not at the helm; his uncle keeps his pills locked in the cabin and would never trust him with the wheel. At Landfall he recognizes the horizon peak by peak. His first reaction is joy, the first vindication of his life: an impossible thing another person can finally see. His cover lie, "I don't know," is the oldest lie he owns, and Landfall is the first time it weighs nothing. The First Witness achievement reads "You were awake. You always are."

**What was done to him (canon; depiction rules below are absolute):** A diagnosed, disbelieved, medicated child in a state institution is maximally vulnerable, because his testimony has no value, and predators understand what that means. During the first stint, beginning around age 11, a staff orderly abused him, physically and sexually. He reported it once. The report was logged as delusional ideation and his dosage was increased. He never asked to be rescued again. What he did instead is the point.

**The war (canon, with the handler):** The failed report taught him rescue was never coming. The next lesson had a teacher. **Mười Bạc** ("Gambler Ten"; Tuan calls him Chú Mười) arrived on the ward when Tuan was 12: a bookmaker gaming a court-ordered evaluation to dodge sentencing, a sane man hiding among the disbelieved, the inversion of Tuan. He noticed the boy's "ravings" kept coming true, tested him quietly, and opened a book on him: lô đề numbers, match scores, discharge dates, whether it would rain on inspection day, run through a smuggled phone and a runner cousin outside. (Localization lock: no Kalshi or Western prediction markets; the underground lô đề and bookie economy is the authentic mechanism.) The trade was never fair: precision cost Tuan pain, and Mười learned to ration the boy's medication like a handler, sharpening him for query days and soothing him after. But Mười also taught him everything: how staff think, what gets a man fired versus ignored, how to trade, how to be small, how to read an institution the way a bookmaker reads a fixed match. The Arranger campaign was their first joint operation, Tuan supplying the futures and Mười the institutional physics, and abuser number one left in a police car, convicted for the theft, never for the truth. First doctrine, taught and learned: you don't need to be believed to be lethal, you need other people to be believable. The second predator was careful and stole nothing, and one evening Tuan foresaw Mười pushing him down the north stairwell, and said nothing, and watched it arrive, and then told Mười exactly where to be and what to be doing when the questions came. The man lived, wheeled out on disability. The investigation found nothing, because the boy who could see the investigation had edited it in advance. That night cemented them. Mười was released when Tuan was 13 and kept the book running by phone; after Tuan's discharge at 14, Mười's network supplied work, cover, and protection. The family's price for retrieving him after the second hospitalization was the uncle's boat, far from Mười's orbit, and Mười allowed it the way a man allows a deposit to sit in a bank.

**Why Mười matters later (locked as future thread):** at Landfall, somewhere in the HCMC underworld is a bookmaker holding years of winning receipts that prove a true seer exists, in a world that just acquired a System. He will come looking for his oracle. His relationship to Nam Cam's network is open; decide in Book 2 planning.

**THE UNHEARD DOCKET (Tuan's office; locked):** Unique-grade titles carry offices, not just buffs, and First Witness plus Logged Anyway qualify Tuan as court-appointed counsel for the disbelieved. On the court's schedule ("the Docket calls when it calls; scheduling is the court's"), he receives an appointment: a being somewhere in the multiverse who is telling the truth and is surrounded by people who think they are broken. The System knows they are sane, because it believes the record over the reviewer, but belief is outside its jurisdiction, so it appoints advocates from the registry of the vindicated. Sessions convene in vision-space during sleep, which for a chronic insomniac means his only reliable rest is literally court-appointed. Helping pays on a fee schedule scaled to significance: stat grants, Anguish rebates (feeding the Pain Eater track), favors banked, and precedent fame, since his name then appears as "counsel of record" in other beings' award citations across the multiverse. The summons text is canon: "Appointment: Counsel of Record. The following being is telling the truth. No one in their life believes them. You are qualified. Fee schedule attached. Declining is permitted. Counsel was appointed to your case once. Counsel declined." When Tuan accepts, the System answers with its standard line: "Your choice has been logged." Sample clients (seeds, not locks): a young star-whale who keeps insisting the ocean ends (its pod calls it sick; it is approaching the world-rim); a goblin accountant who alone believes the ledgers are haunted (they are); a god's herald who reported that the god is dead (nobody believes him; the god is dead, and the prayers are being answered by an automation). His first advocate was Mười, who believed him for profit; the Docket makes Tuan the Mười who does not take a cut. **Usage cap (binding): at most one Docket case per arc, and every case must braid into the main plot; the Docket is a thematic engine, never episodic filler.**

**The record:** the account "records your body, your deeds, and your debts." At Landfall, Tuan's backdated cascade includes entries he never spoke aloud, with dates, and with a name. The boy nobody believed holds the multiverse's notarized record. The cold open line "Objections: logged anyway" is, on reread, his line. The first abuser was convicted, but for the theft, never the truth; the true entry exists only in the cascade. What he does with the record, testimony rather than revenge, is a Landfall-arc decision (open; names withheld until then).

**Manifestation (the Sarah Connor principle, per Cameron: not crazy, but changed by what he experienced):** at 16 he sleeps with his back to walls, hoards food, reads every room for its exits and its levers, and distrusts kindness because kindness was always an opening move. He is quiet the way a drawn bow is quiet. The history reaches the page as vigilance and leverage, never as cowering.

**The two sins (revised, locked):** His sins are the moral costs of a sane child's survival war, never violent psychosis; the crazy-equals-violent conflation remains banned.
1. **Selection (age 13).** The stairwell. He foresaw the push, chose the future where it happened by saying nothing, and then taught the pusher how to vanish from the record. Not the act, and not even relief: the discovery that editing outcomes worked, felt clean, and left nothing even a god could argue with, and that part of him has been looking for a reason to do it again ever since. The appetite is the sin.
2. **The Quiet (age 14).** His doctrine ran on disciplined silence, aimed at enemies, and then it caught a friendly: he saw a ward-mate's death date and his discipline held. She died on schedule, alone, and the spotless record his silence built became his discharge. The weapon he forged for predators fired through an innocent, and he walked free on the recoil.

**Title chain (backdated cascade processed at Landfall; each entry pays concrete rewards per screen discipline rule 7):** The Warned (childhood, first true vision) → Unheeded ("Awarded for delivering a true warning at personal cost. It did not help. They rarely do.") → **Logged Anyway** (the failed report: "You reported it once. It is the System's policy to believe the record over the reviewer. Entry retained. Name retained.") → **House Odds** (the ward book: "You were the edge. He was the book. The System declines to state which of you profited more.") → **The Arranger** (the fired-and-arrested abuser: "You could not be believed, so you made other people believable. The System notes the efficiency.") → **Selection** (the stairwell: "You never touched him. You chose the future where another man did, then edited the aftermath. The System keeps better records.") → Keeper of the Quiet (the silence that caught a friendly) → Witness of Phu Nhuan (the massacre; the title that binds him to the party by name) → First Witness (Landfall, canon in the cold open). Class track: Prophet, evolving at an accumulated-suffering threshold into **Pain Eater**.

**Pain Eater kit:** Resource: **Anguish**, banked from pain endured, witnessed, or absorbed. Core ability: **Take**, drawing the pain (never the damage) out of another person and holding it; allies fight on wounded and feeling nothing, which is support power and mortal danger in one mechanic. Anguish funds his foresight: precision costs pain, his own or carried. The closed loop is the tragedy: eat pain to fund visions, visions bring more grief. Eating pain is never healing trauma; he is a reservoir, and the series respects what reservoirs eventually do.

**Guardrails (binding, from the pathologized-seer research):** Both the visions and the damage are real. Medication was suppression AND survival, never simply villainous. Vindication is partial, late, and reintroduces burden: the backdated cascade enrages as much as it heals, and off the meds the visions return in force. No tidy absolution: his sins rhyme with the party's massacre, and that rhyme, guilt met with guilt, is the glue when he joins them. He is a survivor, not a revenge fantasy: the humanity anchors are the Quiet's collateral, the Phu Nhuan mismatch (a doctrine built for slow institutional war, useless at the speed of gunfire), and his fear of his own appetite for Selection. Whatever-it-takes is scoped to survival, never sadism, and none of it is ever attributed to illness. **Mười is never laundered:** he groomed a drugged child's talent and profited from queries that cost the boy pain, AND he is the only adult who ever treated Tuan as valuable and taught him to survive. Both stay true on the page; nostalgia and indictment travel together. **Depiction rules for the abuse, absolute and series-long:** never depicted in scene, no graphic description anywhere, ever; conveyed only through implication, aftermath, testimony, and System records; never his defining trait, one wound in a ledger of them. This is the research's no-spectacle rule, it is what makes the restrained versions land, and it is also a hard platform requirement (graphic depiction violates Royal Road and Amazon content policy outright).

**Retrofit ban:** Tuan is NOT visible in Chapter 2. He was one bystander among hundreds at the market. The reveal arrives as his testimony in the Landfall arc, never as an inserted detail in the massacre chapter.

> **Quest: Blood Debt.**
> 19 lives were taken that owed you nothing.
> Repayment schedule: to be determined by the survivor.
> Interest accrues.

The System converts the massacre into a permanent, uncapped questline adjudicated by Chit-tat-to. He decides what counts as repayment. ## 9. VOICE GUIDE: THE THREE REGISTERS

1. **The Auditor (System):** dry, precise, procedurally cruel, involuntarily humane. Section 6 rules. Carries page-level comedy so the prose doesn't have to reach for jokes.
2. **Griffin register (human chapters):** competent professionals being understated about horrifying things, rendered the way Griffin actually writes: procedure EXPLAINED with patient, dry precision, never clipped into insider shorthand. Griffin's hallmark is walking the reader through the stance, the weapon, the protocol ("he assumed a boxer's firing stance, one foot back, both hands on the Beretta" is the mode; "front sight, press" is not). The explanation itself carries the authority; clarity IS the register, so jargon is never exempt from self-glossing. Humor from procedure, rank friction, and gallows economy. Banter is earned by competence. The Champion/Companion rank inversion between the siblings is this register's renewable fuel. Correction logged from Tony's catch: earlier drafts conflated Griffin with clipped-jargon thriller shorthand; that conflation is banned.
3. **Brooks register (Chit-tat-to chapters):** warmth, wonder, fable-cadence, big-hearted found-family adventure, with grief underneath. His chapters may be gentler and slightly shorter. They are the palate cleanser that makes the dark chapters land harder.

**Hard bans (Tony's voice rules + genre-punished patterns):** no em/en dashes; no rhetorical questions in narration; no "not X but Y" constructions; no dramatic colons; minimal adjectives; no character introduced via height/weight stats (physicality through action only); no reference-joke humor; no head-hopping (POV changes at chapter breaks only); no lore as documents; no repeated notification spam.

**Craft rules added from Tony's Ch 1-4 notes (apply to every chapter going forward):**
1. **First-line rule.** Every chapter, and every major POV or scene shift inside a chapter, opens on motion, sensation, or threat. Never a wry framing observation. Applies with maximum force to the first line of the book and the first line of every book. Test: a tired reader on a phone keeps scrolling.
2. **Banter clarity.** A joke must land on first read. If the gag requires re-reading the previous line to decode, rewrite it explicit or cut it.
3. **No unearned weight, no image-riddles.** A line only gets gravitas the scene has already banked. If a sentence is reaching for depth (the "sidearm and newspapers" failure), it either gets set up properly or it dies. Same for sensory observations: if the image requires decoding ("Distance should have made it soft. It was not soft."), replace it with the concrete version of what it was reaching for ("Land that far away should have been a blue smudge on the sky. Tuan could count the peaks.") or cut it. When in doubt, go lighter and more literal.
4. **Miss Hue is the calibration reference.** Her Ch 1-4 register (dry authority, patience as a weapon, jokes that are also verdicts) is confirmed correct. When unsure how a scene should sound, ask what Miss Hue would do to it.
5. **Read-aloud check.** Every chapter gets a repetition and cadence pass as if narrated (this book is audio-first). Doubled words in close proximity, stacked trailing clauses, and passive constructions get caught here.
6. **Vietnamese term convention.** Character and place names romanize without diacritics for reader ease (Diep, Cuong, Phu Nhuan, Bien Dong). Cultural terms italicize with full diacritics on first use and carry an organic gloss (*tất niên*, "the party that sees the old year out before Tết"; *lô đề*). Glosses read as apposition a Vietnamese reader would not find condescending, never as encyclopedia entries.
7. **Dramatize, never report.** Cool content is never summarized ("she had hunted elk with a bow in Mongolia, and she was not above mentioning it" is the banned form). If a story, feat, or history is worth the reader's time, it goes on the page as scene: dialogue, action, texture. Oft-told family stories are delivered as call-and-response, characters completing each other's sentences and correcting details, which shows intimacy while the content lands. If it is not worth dramatizing, cut it entirely. There is no middle register.

**Canonized in the cold open (lore now locked):** worlds customarily receive one century of notice before integration; Earth's century was spent hidden by Adonai's decision, and the System says so publicly ("The decision has been logged. He is sorry."). Adonai's culpability is on the record from page one, unnamed until Chapter 3.

**Per research:** the dry-military register is the least-proven bet in the genre. Validation gate: chapters 1-10 go to beta readers before we write past chapter 20. If the Griffin register reads as low-energy, we warm the human chapters toward DCC-standard banter and keep Griffin concentrated in Cuong.

## 11. SERIES MAP (SKETCH, FLEXIBLE)

**11.5 POWER CURVE DOCTRINE (locked, from Tony's design; System Universe as model and cautionary tale):** Cuong goes deep in the hole at Ch 5 (severe garnishment, exact fraction set in drafting, plus Debtor visibility) and climbs as a badass through Book 1 into early Book 2: the fighting genius at level nothing, winning on skill while the numbers starve. **The Escrow:** garnished XP is held, not destroyed; the reader watches his true level accrue in a locked account; full satisfaction at the end of Book 2 releases principal plus restoration interest, detonating him and the party into OP mode with every point pre-witnessed. ## COLD OPEN: LANDFALL

*One year from now.*

The horizon had grown a coastline.

There were no fish in the Bien Dong that night, the wide sea the world's maps call the South China. The nets had come up empty three times, and toward midnight Captain Phan Van Loi had pointed the bow at better water and told his crew to sleep, because the diesel was burning either way.

A crewman held the wheel on the straight heading, an old man with a face like a dried fig, steering with his chin on his chest.

Tuan, sixteen, the captain's nephew, taken aboard as a family favor nobody discussed, his pills kept in a locked drawer in the captain's cabin, was awake in the bow because he could not sleep. He had rarely ever been able to. He was a thin boy, small for sixteen, five years of institution food and a year of ship work in him, with arms that had learned to haul nets.

That is how the least important man on the boat became the first to see it.

He did not wake the captain. The pills made the things he saw small and far away, like weather on another coast, and he waited for this one to fade the way the others always faded. It did not fade. He would say later that he had spent five years learning what happens to a boy who reports things that cannot be there, and that he had wanted to be very sure.

The coastline ran north and south past both edges of the world, gray at the waterline, then green, then white at the mountaintops in the first sun. The chart pinned in the wheelhouse said there was no land within two days of this water.

Tuan did not need the chart to know the shape on the horizon. He had been drawing that skyline since he was nine, on intake forms and the backs of worksheets, and the doctors had a word for a boy who draws the same imaginary coast year after year.

There was surf breaking on it now. He could count the peaks, and he knew them the way he knew his own name.

His uncle came up the ladder with his coffee, a squat weathered man who had run this boat for thirty years, and froze with one foot still on the rung.

On the depth sounder, the numbers that told him how much water lay under the boat were falling like a dropped stone. Three hundred meters. Then two hundred. Then ninety.

It meant the sea floor was climbing toward them out of water that had been two kilometers deep on every chart he had ever owned. It meant that if they held this heading they would run aground on a coast that had not been there an hour ago.

Birds streamed overhead, more than either of them had ever seen, all flying the wrong way, out to sea, screaming.

Tuan was not watching the coastline anymore. He was watching his uncle see it. His whole life, no one else had ever seen the things he saw. That was the entire problem with him, according to everyone who had ever been paid to have an opinion. Now one of those things stood in the open ocean with surf breaking on it, his uncle was staring straight at it, and the feeling rising in Tuan's chest was so unfamiliar it took him a breath to recognize it as joy.

"What country is that?" his uncle said, smaller than Tuan had ever heard him.

"I don't know," Tuan said.

It was the coastline from his drawings, peak for peak. He had known it since he was nine. He had said *I don't know* to doctors and to his own mother for so long that it came out on its own.

Then, in front of both their eyes, hanging over the water like the afterimage of a flash, blue-bordered and patient, appeared writing.

> **World Event: Landfall.**
> Your world's exemption expired at dawn, local time. Enrollment: universal. Consent: not required. Objections: logged anyway.
> Worlds customarily receive one century of notice before integration. Yours was spent hidden, by the decision of a third party. The decision has been logged. He is sorry.
> An account has been opened in your name. It records your body, your deeds, and your debts. You may inspect it at any time by thinking the words "status screen."
> You are Level 1. Almost everyone is.
> Estimated time to impact: you have already run out.

And in front of the boy alone, a second message, smaller, addressed.

> **Achievement: First Witness.**
> Of eight billion, you saw it first. You were awake. You always are.
> Grade: Unique. One per world. This will never be awarded again.
> Precedent: this award has issued on 1,406 worlds.
> Most recent prior recipient: Szel of Vekh-Amun, subsequently First Navigator of the Unbroken Fleet.
> Outcomes are not guaranteed.
> Awarded: Wisdom +3. Luck +3.
> Skill recognized: **Horizon Eyes** (Unique). You have had this for some time. The System apologizes for the delay in documentation.
> Backdated entries: processing. There are many.
> The System suggests you remember this day. It will.


## CHAPTER 1: MARKET DAY

*One year earlier. Ho Chi Minh City.*

The kettle had no business screaming in the finals.

Diep Loc was a point up with thirty seconds on the match clock, and Trang's knife kept coming, edge dulled for competition, every touch a point.

Trang had a head of height and longer arms, the way everyone did, and Diep had spent twelve years turning that into a joke at their expense, going in low and fast under arms that always arrived a half-count late.

Somewhere above the packed bleachers of the Phu Tho sports hall a kettle was screaming and no one else could hear it. Not the referee. Not the two thousand people in the stands.

The sound pulled her eyes up for half a beat, and Trang needed exactly that long. The touch landed on her ribs and the scoreboard flipped, and the crowd began chanting Trang's name, and the kettle screamed on and on until she was awake and swinging.

She caught the lamp with the last swing and sent it off the nightstand. It hit the floor with a crash that was enormous in the small room, and she sat in the wreck of her blankets, guard up, heart going, defending her bedroom from furniture.

"Are you fighting someone in there?" Cuong asked through the door.

"No."

"Did you win?"

Her heart was still going. The lamp was on the floor. "What time is it?"

"Six forty. I have been dressed for shift for an hour, and you have been losing to your blanket for most of it." A pause. "Was it Trang? You already beat Trang."

That was how her brother comforted people, by correcting the record. Diep looked at the trophy on its shelf and didn't answer. With Cuong, she never had to.

She dressed while he waited. The romper was orange with white lotus flowers, a gift from their mother, worn today as strategy, because their mother would hear about the party tonight and ask what she wore, and certain questions were better answered in advance.

It left her arms bare, which meant the whole market would see the yellow bruise on the left forearm where Trang had gotten through on Sunday, the one souvenir of a final she had won. Red canvas shoes, the good ones. By the time she opened the door, backpack on one shoulder, the old green one that had survived three years of tournaments, she had herself back.

"University students are legally entitled to sleep past seven," she said. "It's in the constitution."

"I'll raise it with the National Assembly."

"Raise it from the market. We need corn, sweet potatoes, and if Auntie Bay has good bananas, bananas."

Her brother was already in uniform, filling the hallway the way he filled doorframes, shoulders and forearms built by twenty years of throws and grappling on a hard mat. Their mother said he had the face of their father and none of his patience.

The tất niên was tonight, the party that sees the old year out before Tết, and it was her first with her own classmates at her own university.

Mei, her roommate, had said once over instant noodles that Diep's family made the best grilled corn in Phu Nhuan. She had said it in passing, the way people say things they forget by morning. Diep had not forgotten.

She had signed up to bring corn for forty people, and the corn was why she had slept in her old room last night. The recipe was her mother's. The market was her family's. She had spent a week planning this run, because Mei was the first friend she had made on her own, outside the training hall and outside her brother's shadow.

Miss Hue, their neighbor from the end of the hall, was at the elevator, a market bag of knotted string over one arm, white hair in its knot, waiting with the stillness of a woman who had outlived every reason to hurry.

"There they are," she said. "I read the newspaper this week."

"Here we go," Cuong said.

"Both sparring divisions." Miss Hue nodded to each of them, formal as a judge. "And the weapons division." She let it sit. She was ninety-two years old and she let it sit the way a fisherman lets a line sit. "Diep, I cut out the article. You look very fierce in the photograph. Cuong is also in the photograph. In the background. Watching."

"I was proud of her," Cuong said.

"Second place is nothing to be ashamed of," Miss Hue said, stepping into the elevator.

"I got third."

"Even braver of you to go out in public."

Diep offered the old woman her elbow and did not laugh, because holding it in was more fun.

It had been Miss Hue, back when Cuong was seven, who marched into their parents' shop and announced that both children would train at the Vovinam school on Phan Dang Luu. Vovinam was Vietnam's own martial art, empty hands and blades in one system, founded in Hanoi during the French occupation by a Vietnamese master who believed his people should not have to borrow their fighting from anyone.

Miss Hue had been one of the children in his earliest classes, and she had not stopped at classes. In the war against the French, the art was used for what it had been built for, and she had been one of the ones using it.

The old men at the school called her Chị Ba, Sister Three. When Diep was nine she had asked why, and every one of them had suddenly needed to be somewhere else.

Training would keep the siblings close and keep them Vietnamese, Miss Hue said, and she went to see the master about the fee. The master poured her tea with both hands and refused to take one.

She had been correct on every count. She generally was.

Six years sat between the siblings, and the training hall had closed that gap, two of them against the world every Sunday morning until their forearms bruised in matching places.

The walk took twenty minutes and Miss Hue filled it.

"Did I ever tell you about Mongolia," she said, which was not a question.

"The year our father was born," Diep said.

"You hunted elk," Cuong said. "With a bow."

"With a bow I made," Miss Hue corrected. "From an ash stave and boiled sinew, because the guide's bows were toys. Go on, since you know it so well."

"The elk went down in the river," Diep said. "You went in after it."

"In October," said Cuong.

"In October," Miss Hue agreed, satisfied, as if the month were the whole point, and stepped around a parked motorbike without looking at it. "Your generation swims in heated pools and calls it exercise."

The sidewalk ran shoulder to shoulder, exhaust and cigarettes and someone's soup stock on the air, motorbikes six deep at the light. Miss Hue stopped in front of a coffee shop with a neon cactus buzzing in the window.

"This was a rice field," she said, to the cactus.

"The cactus didn't do it," Diep said.

"It benefited." Miss Hue moved on.

"Will Mei be at the party tonight?" Miss Hue asked, who missed nothing on their floor and considered it a duty.

"She's not answering her phone," Diep said. "A week now."

Miss Hue looked at Cuong. She said nothing. It was the way she had looked at the cactus.

"I was polite to her," Cuong said.

"You looked her family up in the police database," Diep said. "Politely."

"You were moving in with a stranger. I checked. I had the tools, so I used them."

"And then you couldn't look at her the same way. Over some secondhand phones."

Cuong walked a few steps with his jaw set. "I'll fix it," he said. "Tell her I want to say something to her. Properly, in front of you, whenever she'll stand still for it."

"Apologies age like fish," Miss Hue said.

"Tonight, then. Before your party." He looked at Diep. "If she'll come to the door, I'll say it at the door."

Diep was still mortified about it, four days later. On Sunday after the tournament, Cuong had carried her gear bag up to the dorm room, still glowing about his bronze like it was gold. Mei's family photo stood on her desk, her father and two brothers crowded around her in front of a shop grate, everyone squinting into the sun. Cuong picked it up before anyone could stop him.

"Good photo," he said. "Your dad's the phone guy by the university, right?" And then, because the day had made him expansive, "We know him at the station, actually. Phones that weren't exactly his to sell. Nothing serious, though."

Mei had known he was police for a month, since his academy photo crossed her feed. Knowing it and hearing him recite her father's record in her own room were different things. Even Cuong noticed the quiet. He set the photo down and tried to joke his way out of it. "Somebody's got to check out my little sister's roommate," he said, too cheerful and too late. "You passed."

Diep had laughed. It had half sounded like a joke, and she had wanted it to be one, and she had been hearing that laugh ever since. Mei smiled, said something polite, and had not answered one of Diep's messages since.

The smell of frying oil reached her first, spring rolls somewhere ahead of them, and Diep let Sunday go. The noise rose next, motorbike horns and a hundred bargaining voices, and the market swallowed them.

She spotted Duy at the far vendor, and spotting Duy was never hard. Her brother's partner since the academy was built like a greyhound and talked like a wedding host, wiry and always mid-gesture, and he had the spring roll auntie laughing so hard she was adding extras to his bag with her own hand. Two oil-spotted bags for a one-man order, the bonus you got for being charming. Cuong thought before he spoke. Duy spoke to find out what he thought. Cuong raised a hand.

Then Diep saw Mei.

She was at the fruit stall by the north entrance. Small and neat, dark hair cut short to save time, holding a dragonfruit she was not looking at.

Diep knew those hands. They had opened the dorm room in four seconds the night she locked her keys inside, and Mei had never explained where a person learns that.

Her weight was balanced like a fighter waiting on a bell. Her eyes moved on a circuit that kept passing over Diep and would not land.

Diep raised her hand to wave. Mei looked at her then, fully, and what was in her face was an apology.

Behind her, near Duy, three men in white tank tops stopped walking at the same time and reached behind their backs.


## CHAPTER 2: THE MASSACRE AT PHU NHUAN

The market exploded with violence.

No warning. No demand. One moment Cuong was raising his hand to Duy across the lane, and the next, bullets sprayed into the crowd, sweeping left and right at waist height.

People went down before the sound reached them.

The tall young man who ducked his head every morning to get into the noodle stall took two rounds through the back. His legs kept walking after the rest of him stopped, and he went face first through the stall's front table, and the table came down on top of him along with forty bowls.

The fish seller took one in the hip and one under the arm. She spun into her own counter and folded over it backward, and her brass scale went up out of her hands and came down on the tile beside her head, ringing, spilling her morning's take across the wet stones.

Miss Hue was forty meters away with her market bag on her arm. A burst meant for somebody else walked across her and took the top of her head off. Her body stayed upright a moment longer, hand still closed on the bag, and then the strings went and she dropped straight down where she stood.

Chị Ba, who had fought the French, killed by a stray round on her way to buy fish.

A bullet went through Cuong's left shoulder and put him down in the fruit stall.

He landed hard among the dragonfruit, legs tangled in a crate.

Six months ago he had traded fire with two men in a stairwell in District 8, and it had gotten him the detail, and he had walked around ever since believing he knew what a gunfight was.

He had never been shot before. That was the part nobody could teach him. It went through him like a truck and kept going, and for a few seconds he was not a police officer at all, just a young man lying in fruit with his mouth open, trying to work out where his arm had gone.

Two meters away, Mei hit Diep at the waist and took her off her feet and out of the burst, both of them going down behind the stall's low wall.

Cuong got his right elbow under himself and thought, with the last clean part of his mind, that Mei had just saved his sister's life.

Across the lane, through stalls going over and a stampede of running legs, Cuong found Duy. The first burst had caught him through the hip, and he was hauling himself toward the spring roll vendor's steel table on one elbow, his other hand clamped over the hole, leaving a wet track across the tile behind him.

Alive, then. Moving.

Cuong took stock the way the trainers drilled it, and got about half of it.

Arm dead from the shoulder down. Bullet through and out the other side. Not going to die of it in the next minute, which was as far ahead as he could see.

His right hand was shaking hard enough that he had to look at it twice to be sure it was still holding the Beretta. The left hand was not going to help hold anything, which meant one-handed from here, the thing the range drills prepare you for and hope you never need.

He keyed the radio one-handed and gave the call in the flat voice they trained into you, shots fired, Phu Nhuan central market, officers down, and the word officers came out plural because the arithmetic was already visible from where he lay.

Duy was firing around the steel table one-handed, the other hand still clamped on his hip, face gone gray, grin gone with it. The three gangsters were walking their fire into his cover, each burst chewing a little closer, with the patience of men who had done this before.

Cuong got his right elbow onto a crate, the brace they teach for a dead support arm, and went to work.

The lead man crossed the lane at a run. Cuong shot him three times in the chest. He sat down against a vegetable cart like a drunk.

The second turned to find the new threat. Cuong shot him twice in the back. No honor owed.

The third went to ground behind an overturned cart and did not come up.

One breath. Screaming, and sirens somewhere too far away.

He turned his head to find them behind the low wall, his sister and the girl who had pulled her down.

"Mei," he said. "Thank you. Thank you for"

Diep was not moving. Mei knelt over her with both hands low on Diep's stomach, the way you kneel to hold pressure on a wound. Blood pumped out around her fingers. Her right hand still held the knife.

"I'm so sorry," Mei said. She was crying. She did not let go of the knife. "They have Van."

Cuong shot her twice and felt nothing at all, and understood dimly that the nothing was a wound of its own, and that he was not going to live long enough for it to matter.

He crawled. It took a very long time, longer than the shooting had taken, his legs working and his left side dragging and his hand slipping in the wet, and when he got there he put his good hand where Mei's hands had been and pressed down.

Diep's eyes tracked him. Her mouth made the shape of his name, and blood came instead of it.

"I know," he said. "I know. I'm here. I've got you."

The third gangster came out from behind his cart when the shooting stopped, and crossed the lane at a walk.

Duy came up from behind the steel table with his gun out and blood sheeting down his leg, shouting something that was a warning or a promise. The gangster turned and put three rounds into him at four meters. Duy folded against the steel and stayed there.

Then the man turned and walked toward the fruit stall, taking his time about it.

Cuong watched him come and stayed where he was. His hand was on his sister. He was very cold, and his fingers had gone clumsy, and blood kept coming up around them however hard he leaned.

The first round hit him under the collarbone and turned him halfway over. The second went into his chest and stayed there.

The sky over Phu Nhuan went a beautiful hard blue.

Cuong dragged his hand back onto his sister. It was already too late to matter and he pressed down anyway. She breathed in small wet pieces underneath it. His last thought was not a prayer and was addressed to no one in particular.

Let it take me first. I don't want to see.

It took him first.


## CHAPTER 3: THE VOID

Diep sat up on a slab of marble and the first thing she did was put both hands to her stomach.

The wound was gone. The romper was whole, no blood, no cut, the lotus flowers as white as the morning her mother gave it to her. She pushed her fingers into the place where the knife had gone in, hard, and felt nothing but her own stomach. She did it again. Then she looked at her hands, and her hands were clean, and she wiped them on the romper anyway.

Light stood over her in a column, and beyond it, dark with no walls in it. Four more slabs, four more columns. Cuong, already on his feet, whole, staring at his own chest. Duy, sitting with his head in his hands. Miss Hue, cross-legged, hands folded, lips moving in prayer, market bag gone. And Mei.

Diep came off the slab.

She crossed the space without any memory of deciding to. Mei was still getting to her feet when Diep hit her, and the nose crumpled and went sideways under her knuckles, and Mei dropped.

They were not children.

Snouts. Teeth in the snouts. Fur in reds and grays, naked tails dragging, eyes catching the torch flame in flat coins of green and yellow. Rags for clothes. In the front rank, blades of crude dark iron, and two long spears, leveled.

You watched, and you let five terrified people find out what those cells were the hard way, and where I am from, we have a word for an officer who does that with a warrant."

Miss Hue said, softly, in Vietnamese, "Careful, boy."

Odin considered him. Wind moved the fur at his shoulders. Somewhere below the terrace, night birds Cuong had no names for were saying things to each other in a forest older than his species.

"There he is," Odin said at last, to no one, satisfied about something. "The one who stands up. The bird said so and I doubted." He turned and set the spear toward the mountain's shoulder, toward a pass where the pale stone road went up between broken pillars. "Walk with me. All of you. There is a hall at Threshold where the classing is done, and you will want what it gives you, because the court has posted Vietnam's claim, and the claim will not wait for your grief." He paused, and did not turn around. "Nothing waits for grief. That is not the court. That is everything."

They gathered their little gear. At the door of the keeping place, Cuong stopped and looked back once, down the dark corridor toward a doorless cell, and Odin's voice came from up the road without kindness or cruelty, a report from an old front.

"He died holding a gate against a warden twice his weight, with a ruined arm, laughing," the god said. "In nine thousand years I have furnished halls with less. Now walk."


## CHAPTER 10: THE ROAD AND THE PRICE

The waystation keeper's smile died in the exact second his eyes went above Cuong's head.

It was the third day on the pale road, and the station was the first roofed place they had seen, a low stone house with a fire going and a stable smell and two travelers already at the hearth, tall gray-skinned people who stopped talking when the humans came in. The keeper was a round, quick-eyed man of the same gray people, and he had begun the professional warmth of innkeepers everywhere, and then he read Cuong, and the warmth shut like the cashbox.

"Beds are six marks," he said. To Diep. Only to Diep. "For him, twenty. Debtors pay surety on the linens."

The room got quiet in an interested way. The travelers at the hearth had turned to watch, because apparently this was worth watching everywhere in the universe.

Diep walked to the counter. Her mother had priced flowers at a stall for forty years on thin margins and perfect courtesy, and Diep had watched her do it since she was four.

"Six for me," she said pleasantly. "Six for the priestess, and I would keep the priestess happy. Six for the quiet one. And six for my brother, because the mark over his head says the court garnishes him, and the court's rate is nine in ten, and a keeper who prices above the court's own sentence is correcting the court." She let that sit. She had read the judgment thirty times in three days; she could recite it like a lesson. "Is that what I should tell them at Threshold? That the station on the pass corrects the court?"

The keeper looked at her. He looked at Miss Hue, who smiled at him the way she had once smiled at a cactus.

"Six," the keeper said. "Marks in advance."

Later, on the night watch, with the fire down to coals and her brother finally, chemically asleep, Diep sat on the doorstep and did not look at Mei, who had come out soundless the way she did everything, and sat down an arm's length away, which was the distance they lived at now.

"You watch like him," Mei said quietly. "You didn't used to."

"Don't," Diep said. Then, because the road was long and the question had been eating her since the marble room: "They shot you. Your own people. You did everything they wanted and they shot you where I could see it."

Mei was silent long enough that the coals settled twice.

"I knew they would," she said. "I watched them decide it before I ever agreed. Men like that don't leave the knife alive, Diep, the knife is the loose thread. I did the count anyway. Van free, on one side. Me and, and the rest, on the other." Her voice stayed level the way a wire stays level, under tension. "I'm not asking you to forgive the count. I'm telling you I did it with open eyes, because you keep looking at me like I was tricked, and it's worse than that, and you should know it's worse."

Diep watched the coals. In the morning they would climb the last of the pass. Somewhere behind them, under a mountain, Duy lay in a room with no door, and somewhere ahead a court had posted her country's name on a docket, and beside her sat the person she had loved most and armed least, telling her the truth at last, one watch too late for it to save anyone.

"Corn," Diep said finally. "At the tất niên. Forty people. I practiced the recipe five times."

Mei made a sound that had a laugh buried in it somewhere, and put her face in her hands, and neither of them said anything else until the stars moved.


## CHAPTER 11: THE ASSAY

The hall at Threshold took the sound out of everything. The door that closed behind Diep was not the door she had walked through.

One moment the four of them stood in the classing hall under ceilings built for wings, with Odin talking to a clerk-shaped column of light, and the next Diep stood alone on a wooden floor she knew before she understood knowing it. Phan Dang Luu. The training hall. Evening light through the high windows, the smell of mats and liniment, every board of it correct, and the writing hanging where the master's portrait should have been.

> Assay: commenced.
> This is a simulacrum. Nothing here persists. Everything here counts.

The far door of the training hall stood barred, and through its slats she could see fire, and between her and the door were nine people she had never met, civilians, market-clothes, terror on them like sweat, and the writing continued, patient as a proctor.

> The bar lifts only under a two-person guard technique, executed correctly, simultaneously, at both brackets.
> You know the technique.
> None of them do.
> The fire does not care. You have what time you have.

Diep looked at her own hands. Twelve years, and every hour of it spent making her body better, and not one hour spent putting what her body knew into someone else's, and the System had read her to the floorboards, because that was exactly the shape of the hole in her.

She picked the calmest-looking one, a wiry aunty with flour on her sleeves.

"You," Diep said, and heard her own voice shake, and kept going anyway, the way you walk out when the bracket posts. "Look at me, not the fire. Feet like this."

She kicked off her shoes, red canvas, the good ones, because you do not step on a mat shod, her body did it before her mind voted, and the writing flickered small in the corner of her eye.

> Cultural rite recognized: unshod instruction.
> Honest Feet: minor bonus applied.
> The Authors held that the honest walk barefoot. The System declines to explain further.

She had no time to read it twice.

She taught badly, then less badly. Hands on the aunty's wrists. Breathe. Again. Lower. Again. Good. She used the master's old cadence because it was the only teaching voice she had.

The fire came through the far side of the door.

On the ninth attempt the aunty's bracket turned with hers, together, and the bar came up.

Nine strangers who did not exist went through into a cool dark that did not exist either. Diep stood alone in her childhood, barefoot, shaking.

The hall faded. The writing stayed.

> Assay: concluded.
> You were offered strength and chose transmission. This has been the pattern of your line for one hundred years. It is now the pattern of your record.

> **Class offered: THE FOUNDER.**
> Grade: Unique. One per world.
> Precedent: this class has issued on 61 worlds.
> Most recent prior recipient: Ashkava of Drenn, subsequently First Teacher of the Nine Free Cities.
> Outcomes are not guaranteed.
> Mechanic: TRANSMISSION. You may co-author classes for those you train, one rank above your own attainment. Their oaths bind to your School. Their deeds enter your ledger, credits and debts alike.
> Constraint: School-bound. A Founder who abandons her School is reclassified. The word for the reclassification is not shown until relevant.
> Accept?

Diep read it three times. *Their deeds enter your ledger, credits and debts alike.* Somewhere outside the simulacrum, her brother stood under nineteen and five and eleven findings, and she had already signed for him once.

"Accept," she said, and the training hall let her go.


## CHAPTER 12: THE COURT'S OFFICER

Cuong's assay was a door, because of course it was a door.

The simulacrum built itself out of his worst inventory: a corridor, a gate, a crank, and behind him nine strangers with market faces, and coming up the corridor something huge on a schedule. He had one hand, a Beretta with four rounds that his own screens told him would score him almost nothing, and a body the court had priced at one-tenth of itself.

He held the door. That part was never in question, and the assay seemed to know it, because the assay was not about the door.

Halfway through, with the thing in the corridor two turns away, the writing came.

> Observation: your balance impairs you. This is by design.
> Provision: within this assay, balance may be transferred.
> Select any present individual. Your judgments become theirs. Your strength returns in full.
> They are simulacra. Nothing here persists.
> Everything here counts.

Cuong read it once. He looked at the nine market faces, at one aunty with flour on her sleeves, and understood that the court was not asking whether he could hold a door. It was asking what he was, at one-tenth, in the dark, with permission.

"No," he said, and turned back to the corridor, and held the door at the price marked. He was crying while he did it, and his hand shook so badly on the third shot that he missed a target the width of a door, and he kept firing anyway, and kept standing there anyway, one arm and four rounds and a crank and everything the Korean instructors had ever screamed at him. When the thing in the corridor finally filled the last turn he did not feel brave. He felt sick, and small, and absolutely certain that he was not going to move.

The assay ended before it reached him, because the assay had its answer.

> Assay: concluded.
> You were offered relief and declined to price it in other people.
> The court requires officers. It has learned to trust the indebted. They return.

> **Class offered: BAILIFF.**
> Mechanic: INTERPOSE. Once per engagement, an attack that would land on your charge lands on you instead. Distance is not consulted.
> Mechanic: OFFICER OF THE COURT. Your voice carries minor compulsion in matters of custody, surrender, and protection. The compulsion is real. So is the paperwork.
> Constraint: a Bailiff's charge is registered. Yours is already on file. You registered it at age six, at a hospital window.
> Standard issue accompanies the office.
> Accept?

He accepted. It named nothing he had not already been for nineteen years. The only difference was that the multiverse would enforce it now. The hall rebuilt itself around him. Standard issue was waiting on the stone: a short iron stave that drank the light, and a badge of pale metal that settled onto his chest and sank, and above his head, beside the judgments that everyone could read, a new line took its place in the record.

Miss Hue came out of her own door upright and furious and gold-lit, gripping a morningstar that had grown considerably in ambition, and behind her eyes something had been said that she was not sharing. "Priestess," she announced, in the voice of a woman who had lodged her complaint with the specific official and received, apparently, an answer. "We will discuss what he said later. Or never."

Mei came out last and shared nothing. Above her head, where a class should have shown, a single line of fine print held its place, unreadable from any angle, and her face asked no one to try.

At the center of the hall the clerk-light brightened, and the day's business concluded the only way business concluded here.

> Party registered: VIETNAM.
> Champion: Diep Loc, the Founder. Companions: 3 of 4. Roster deficiencies: noted.
> The claim of Vietnam is active. First challenge: docketed.
> Date, venue, and opposing counsel: to follow.
> The court thanks you for your compliance. The court notes that compliance was not optional.

Odin read it over their heads, and the one eye came down to Diep, and for the first time since the marble room, the old god looked almost cheerful, which frightened her more than the docket did.

"Good," he said. "Now you're worth training."

## CHAPTER 13: LANDFALL (SMALL)

The road came down out of the pass and turned into a place where people lived.

Diep smelled it before she saw it. Woodsmoke and animals and cooking fat, and under that the particular sourness of a lot of bodies in a small area with nowhere good to put their waste. She had smelled that in the alleys behind the market at the end of a hot week. It was the first thing on Atlantis that had smelled like home, and it took the top off something she had been holding shut since the terrace.

Then the trees opened.

The town sat in the crook of two hills behind a palisade of split logs, maybe three hundred roofs, thatch and slate mixed. Smoke stood up straight from the chimneys. A water mill turned on the creek at the low end. Beyond it the ground went up again in terraces of pale grass, and further out, blue with distance, the mountains they had come down from.

Ordinary. It was completely ordinary, and Diep had to stop walking.

"Steady," Cuong said beside her.

"It's a town."

"It is."

"There are children." She could see them, three of them, throwing something at a fence and missing. "I don't know what I expected. Nobody told me there would be children."

"Sixteen hundred and change," Odin said, up the road, not turning around. "Give or take a season's dying. Human wedge, eastern frontier, four days' walk to the goblinkind border, which is nearer than the people who live here like to be reminded." He planted the spear and finally looked back at them. "Wall's Rest. Learn its name. You will be paying rent here."

Mei walked at the back with her arm bound across her chest in the sling Miss Hue had made her out of a torn cloak, and she had not spoken since the pass. Her face had gone the colors faces go on the third day. Diep had watched her all morning without meaning to, and every time she caught herself doing it she looked away.

The gate guards were two men with spears and a boy with a hand bell. One of the men saw Odin and went down on one knee so fast his spear rang off the palisade. The other one looked at the god, then at the four humans behind him, and rang for his sergeant.

There was a lot of standing around after that.

Cuong used it. He walked the line of the palisade with his hand on the logs, looking at the ground where it met the wall, the way he had walked fence lines at the academy. Diep followed him because following him was habit.

"Sally port there," he said quietly. "Bad angle. Whoever built this expected an attack from the road and never got one." He crouched at a place where the earth had washed out under a log. "This is where I'd come in."

"You're doing it again."

"I know." He straightened. "It helps."

The sergeant arrived, a heavyset woman with grey in her braid and a livery coat that had been repaired more times than it had been washed. She had the look of a person who had been sergeant of a frontier gate for twenty years and intended to die that way with paperwork in her hand.

She did not kneel to Odin. She nodded, once, the way you acknowledge weather.

"Champions," she said, and it was not a welcome, it was a filing category. "Vietnam. Word came down the wire three days back. You are billeted at the Kettle, second floor, and the town does not feed you free past a week." Her eyes went over them and stopped on the mark above Cuong's head, and stayed there a beat too long. "Ah," she said.

"There's a room ready?" Cuong asked.

"There's a room."

"Then we'll take the wounded up first." He turned to organize it, and his hand came out toward Mei's good elbow, and his voice went into the shape it had held for six years on shift, flat and clear and expecting to be obeyed. "Mei, with me. Diep, stay at the gate with Miss Hue and get the sergeant's brief. Move."

Nothing happened.

Nobody moved, and the not-moving had a texture to it, a small resistance in the air like a held breath, and then the writing came, and it came for all of them at once, which was the part Diep would remember.

> Order refused.
> Companion Cuong Loc is not the party's Champion and cannot direct its members.
> Champion of Vietnam: Diep Loc.
> The System has logged the attempt. The System expects to log several more.

Nobody said anything.

Cuong's hand was still out toward Mei's elbow. He looked at the writing, and then he lowered his hand and put it in his pocket, and the sergeant looked at the ground with the tact of a professional who had seen worse things happen to better people.

Diep's face was hot. "Cuong, I don't"

"Give the order," Cuong said.

"It was a good order."

"It was. Give it."

She said it. She heard herself say it, her own voice in her own mouth, telling her brother to take the wounded upstairs, and she heard how young it sounded in the open air of a frontier town, and everyone did what she said, and that was worse than if they hadn't.

Odin watched the whole thing from the gate with his one eye and said nothing at all, which Diep decided she disliked more than if he had laughed.

---

The Kettle's second floor was one long room with six straw pallets and a window with no glass in it. Miss Hue took the pallet by the door without asking anybody. Mei took the one furthest from everyone and lay down facing the wall.

Diep sat on the sill with her feet in the room and looked out at the town going about its evening.

A man was arguing with another man about a goat. A woman carried water up from the mill with a yoke across her shoulders, two buckets, forty steps, and did not stop to rest. Somebody was singing four houses over, badly, and somebody else told them to stop, and they didn't.

Sixteen hundred people. None of whom knew that the world she came from had eleven months left, and all of whom were about to be defended by a nineteen-year-old university student whose only qualification was that a god had liked her brother's mouth.

Her screen sat at the edge of her vision, patient, folded down to its dot. She opened it.

> Champion of Vietnam: Diep Loc.
> Standing: provisional.
> Claim status: unconfirmed. A claim that is not defended is not a claim.
> Your account has been credited with the Founder's first allotment.
> The System notes that you have not spent it.
> The System notes that you have not asked what it is for.

Diep read that twice.

Down in the street the woman with the yoke reached her doorway, set the buckets down, and stood a moment with her hands on her back.

"What's it for," Diep said, out loud, to nobody.

The writing did not answer.

Behind her, in the dark of the long room, Mei began to cry, quietly, the way you cry when you are trying not to be heard by four people who can all hear you. Nobody went to her. Miss Hue lay on her back with her eyes open and her hands folded on the morningstar, and Cuong sat against the wall with his boots on and his eyes shut, and Diep sat in the window until the singing four houses over stopped for the night.


## CHAPTER 14: CHIT-TAT-TO I: THE VILLAGE OF NINETEEN ABSENCES

Chit-tat-to came home along the top of the water pipe, because the top of the water pipe was the fastest way and he had never once been allowed to use it.

He used it now. Nobody was left who would tell him not to.

The pipe ran from the black spring down through the root tunnels to the village, an arm's width of old stone laid by somebody long dead, and he went along it at a flat run with his tail out for balance and his mother's carry-basket bumping empty against his back. Twice he had to jump gaps where the stone had fallen. He made both jumps. Nobody saw him make them.

He was thinking in pictures, because that was how thinking was done.

The picture in front, the one he was running toward, was the village: the smoke hole, the grinding stone, the place where his mother sat to work. The picture behind him, the one that kept climbing up over his shoulder to be looked at again, was the room in the deep.

It went like this, and he could not make it stop going like this.

The cage door, open. The long dark. The others ahead of him and around him, all of them running, his uncle's hand in his fur pushing him low. The tall shapes at the far end with the light behind them. Then the thunder.

The thunder was not a picture. That was the trouble with it. Everything else in his head was a picture and the thunder was a hole where the pictures should be, a white noise-shape with edges, and inside it his aunt going down, and Ptik going down, and the old grandmother who knew all the songs going down with her arms still out behind her holding two of the small ones, and both of the small ones going down as well.

Nineteen.

He had counted them because somebody had to carry the number home, and he was the one still standing, so it was his to carry.

He came off the pipe at the drop-shaft and went down the knotted root by feel, and the smell of home came up at him, wet clay and cooking mash and the sharp green of the moss they grew on the ceiling, and it was so exactly right that for a moment the last two days did not fit inside the world at all.

Then he came out into the common space and everyone was already looking at him.

They had known for a day and a night. Rats know. The village had eighty-one people in it before and now it had sixty-two, and the ones who could count had counted, and the ones who could not count had looked at the doorway every time a shadow crossed it.

His mother was not in the doorway.

He had known she would not be. He had watched her go down in the room in the deep, third from the left, with a piece of the stone floor in her hand because she had picked up the only weapon there was. He had known it for two days and it had been a picture in his head for two days.

It was different, arriving, and finding the doorway empty.

Old Kesh-ka came to him first, walking on both her sticks, and she did not say anything. She put her hand flat on the top of his head, which is what you do for a child, and Chit-tat-to was fifteen and had run the water pipe and carried the number home, and he stood there and let an old woman hold his head still with one hand while he shook.

---

They did the rites at the low fire that night, and they did them without any bodies, which had never happened before in the memory of anyone living.

The rite is simple. For each absence, someone who knew them draws them on the wall in wet ash. Not their face. Their thing: the shape of what they made, or what they carried, or what they were good at, so that anyone who comes to the wall in a hundred years can look at the marks and know what the village used to have.

Ptik got a fishing line, because Ptik could take three fish out of the black spring on a day nobody else could take one.

His aunt got a knot, the complicated one, the one she tied in the roof-lashings that never slipped.

The old grandmother got a spiral for her songs, and then Kesh-ka went back and put a second spiral inside the first, and everyone made the low sound in their throats, because that meant the songs were now unheld, and would have to be learned again from people who only half remembered them.

Chit-tat-to drew his mother himself.

He drew the grinding stone. He drew it badly, because his hands would not do what he told them, and he had to go back twice to make the circle close. Nobody said anything about it. When he was done Kesh-ka reached past him and added one small mark at the edge of the stone, a dot with a curve over it, which was the mark for a person who taught, and Chit-tat-to had not known that his mother had taught anyone anything, and now there was nobody left to ask about it.

Nineteen marks on the wall.

The wall had four hundred years of marks on it. Nineteen new ones, all in a row, all in one night, was a thing the wall had never had to hold before.

---

The talking happened after.

It happened the way talking always happened, everyone in a ring at the low fire, the fire kept small because of the smoke, and the elders speaking in order of age and everyone else speaking when they had something.

Chit-tat-to had never spoken at the fire in his life. He was fifteen. You spoke at the fire when you had done a thing.

He waited a long time.

Kesh-ka spoke first, and what she said was: deeper. There were tunnels below the black spring that nobody had opened in three generations, and the village was small enough now to fit in them, and the tall folk did not go down where the air was bad. The village had moved before. In her mother's time they had moved twice. You survive by being where the boots are not.

Almost everyone made the sound for agreement.

Chit-tat-to stood up.

The ring went quiet, not out of respect, out of the surprise of the thing, and he stood there with his hands opening and closing and every picture in his head arriving at once and no way to get any of them out of his mouth in an order that would work.

So he did what he could do. He went to the wall, and he pointed at his mother's grinding stone, and then he held up his hand, fingers spread, and closed it and opened it four times short of nineteen and then made the flat sign for *and more*.

Then he pointed at the floor, meaning here, meaning down, meaning the deep tunnels Kesh-ka wanted.

Then he shook his head.

"Then what," said Rul, who was thirty and had lost a brother in the room in the deep, and who said it not unkindly. "You want what, Chit? You want to go up and knock on the tall folk's door and ask them for something?"

Chit-tat-to thought about that.

The tall folk. The thunder. The two of them in the light at the end of the room, the male one shouting, the female one going flat to the ground with her hands over her head. He had seen that. Everyone else had been running and he had been looking, because looking was what he did, and he had seen the female one go down on the floor and cover her head while the thunder was still happening.

That was not the shape a person makes when they are doing a thing on purpose.

He did not have any way to say that to a ring of sixty-two people around a fire. He did not have the words for *on purpose*. He was not sure the village had them.

So he pointed at his mother's mark on the wall, and then he held his hand out flat, palm up, which meant *this is owed*, which is a thing you say about a fishing debt or a share of a harvest.

And then, because it was the only other thing he had, he made the sign for *I will*, which is two fingers walking forward, and there was no sign in the world for the rest of it.

Rul looked at him for a while.

"He's fifteen," somebody said from the back.

"He is," said Kesh-ka, and put her sticks under her and got up, which took a long time, and nobody helped her because you did not. She looked at the wall, at the four hundred years, at the nineteen. "And he came home along the water pipe, which is forbidden, and he did not fall off it."

She turned around.

"We go down," she said. "That is decided. I am old and I will not spend the end of my life arguing with a boy." She looked at Chit-tat-to with her small black eyes. "Go up, then. Go and get what is owed. And when you have it, come and find where we have gone, and do not be surprised if it takes you a while, because we will be hiding properly."

---

He left before light, with his mother's basket and a fishing line that had been Ptik's and no plan of any kind.

At the top of the drop-shaft, where the root tunnel opened onto the surface and the cold came in, the picture-screen that had been following him for two days appeared again in front of his eyes.

It had been showing him the same thing since the room in the deep. A picture of the tall male with the thunder-stick. Under it, a mark he did not know, which he had decided meant *the one who did it*.

Now the picture had changed.

It showed a set of scales, the kind the traders used, the pan on one side heavy with small shapes he understood to be his dead, and the other pan empty.

And under the picture, for the first time in his life, there was a WORD.

Not a picture. A word, a real one, the kind the tall folk used, six marks long, sitting there in the air where he could not fail to see it.

Chit-tat-to sat down on the cold stone at the mouth of the tunnel with his mother's basket on his back and looked at it for a long time.

He did not know what it said.


## CHAPTER 15: THE PRICE OF A ROOM

Odin was gone before light, and he left his instructions with the innkeeper instead of with them, which Diep understood was the lesson.

The innkeeper delivered it over breakfast with the air of a man reciting something he had been made to repeat back twice. "He says: you are not to leave the valley for one month. He says: the boy is to be hit until he stops flinching, and he did not say which boy. He says the girl is to spend what she was given." He put down a pot of something grey and steaming. "He also says you owe me for the room, which he did not pay for, and which he told me to tell you he could have."

"That's a god," Miss Hue said.

"That's most of them, ma'am."

The grey stuff turned out to be barley porridge with fat in it. Cuong ate two bowls standing at the window with his back to the room and his eyes on the street, which was his way of sitting down. Mei ate nothing. Diep put a bowl beside her pallet, and Mei said thank you through the wire of her jaw, and Diep left before either of them had to do anything else about it.

---

The Kettle's common room filled at midday and Diep learned more in two hours of sitting in it than she had in four days of walking behind a god.

Wall's Rest was not one town. It was three towns wearing one palisade, and the seams showed if you watched who sat where.

There were the valley people, who had been here for generations and who came in at noon smelling of animals and left by dark. They talked about water rights and about a bridge, and they had a way of not looking directly at anyone in a livery coat.

There were the coat-wearers, a dozen of them, who worked for a man called the Warden of the Eastern Line who lived four days away in Leath and had never once been seen in Wall's Rest. They ran the wire, the tax, and the gate, and the valley people paid them and hated them with the calm hatred of people who had been paying for a long time.

And there were the newcomers. There were a great many newcomers. They slept in a tent field outside the south wall that had been there long enough for the tents to be repaired with other tents, and they were from three villages further east, and they had come with what they could carry when the border moved.

"Border doesn't move," a valley man said, at the next table, to nobody. "Goblinkind doesn't want this valley. Never has. They want the pass and they want the pass because we shut it."

"Tell it to the tents," said his friend.

Diep listened. She had a copper on the table in front of her that she had earned by helping a woman lift a churn, and she made it last three hours, and nobody talked to her, which was fine, because everybody talked around her.

She learned that a claim was a thing you had to defend and that nobody here had ever seen a champion do it.

She learned that champions came through Wall's Rest maybe twice a year, always going east, and that they were mostly polite and mostly frightening.

She learned, from a drunk under the stairs who was talking to a dog, that a champion party had come through in the spring, seven of them, dark-skinned and speaking a language nobody knew, and they had bought every arrow in the valley and paid over the odds for it.

"Where'd they go?" Diep asked.

The drunk looked at her, and looked at the dog, and the dog offered nothing.

"East," he said. "Everybody goes east." He drank. "Nobody comes back west, though, do they. You'd think one of them would come back west just for the once."

---

The trouble started when she tried to buy rope.

Not for any reason. Cuong had said, over the porridge, in the flat voice, that four days from a border with no rope and no oil and eleven arrows between them was not a plan, and Diep had agreed out loud in front of everyone, and had then discovered that agreeing out loud in front of everyone meant she was the one who went and did it.

The rope seller was a broad woman with a shop that was mostly a shed. She had a good coil of it, two fingers thick, and she named a price, and Diep counted out her allotment coins.

Then the woman looked past her at Cuong, who had come along because he came along, and stopped in the middle of taking the money.

"He with you?"

"He's my brother."

"He's carrying a judgment."

"He's carrying two."

The rope seller put the coil back on the shelf.

"Not in my shop," she said, and she was not being cruel about it, that was the strange part, she said it the way you say a thing that everyone knows. "Nothing personal to you, girl. Debtor takes goods on credit anywhere in this valley, the balance follows the goods. Man's in arrears with the court, the court can come at what he's touched." She wiped her hands. "I sold a plough to a debtor eleven years ago. I am still paying for that plough."

"He's not buying it. I am."

"He's standing there."

Diep looked at the rope. She looked at her brother, who had already turned to go, because he had been turning to go since the sergeant's *ah* at the gate.

"Wait outside," Diep said.

He waited outside. She bought the rope, and paid four coppers more than the first price because the woman had gone nervous, and she carried it out herself, and neither of them said one word about it all the way back up the hill.

---

That evening she found the sergeant on the gate and asked for work.

The sergeant's name was Bellim. She had a stool she did not sit on, a ledger she never closed, and a way of looking at people that Diep recognized from every referee she had ever fought under.

"Champions want paid work," Bellim said. "That's new."

"We want to eat past the week."

"Mm." Bellim turned a page in the ledger without looking at it. "There's a thing. It's not a champion's thing. It's a nuisance."

"Tell me the nuisance."

"Sheep." Bellim let that sit, watching her. "Upper terraces. Something is taking sheep, and it is taking them in a way that means it is not a wolf, and the men who went up on the seventh have not come down. That is nine days. Their wives have come to me twice." She turned another page. "The Warden's office says it is a stock matter and stock matters are the valley's own affair. So it is nobody's, so it is yours, if you want it. Two silver, paid out of the town's purse, which I keep in a box, which I will show you is not very full."

Diep thought about the tents outside the south wall and about the woman with the yoke and forty steps and no rest.

"Two silver," she said.

"And you tell me true what you find," Bellim said. "Not what makes a story. I do not need a story. I have three hundred sheep and eleven men on this wall and I need to know what is on my terraces."

"Yes."

Bellim looked at her a moment longer.

"You are how old?"

"Nineteen."

"Mm," said Bellim, and did not say the rest of it, and wrote something in the ledger, and that was the first work the Champion of Vietnam was ever hired to do.

---

Diep told them in the long room with the window, standing up, because sitting down to give an order had felt wrong the one time she tried it.

"Sheep," Cuong said.

"Nine days of missing men."

"Sheep," said Miss Hue, from her pallet, with enormous satisfaction. "Good. You will make every mistake you are going to make, and you will make them where the stakes are sheep, and then later when the stakes are not sheep you will already have made them." She sat up. "I have watched two wars start with someone's goats. Do not let your brother go first."

"He's going to go first."

"Then say it before he does, so it is an order and not a habit."

Diep looked at Cuong. Cuong looked back at her with the mild attentive expression of a man who has been waiting four days to be given something to do.

"You go first," she said. "But you stop when I say stop, and you don't get in front of Mei."

"Mei has one arm."

"That's why."

Something moved at the far end of the room. Mei had rolled over on her pallet and was looking at them, at Diep, with her wrecked face, and her mouth opened as if she was going to say something.

She did not say it. She lay back down and faced the wall.

The screen came up in front of Diep's eyes as she was pulling on her boots, unasked.
